To the Strait-Jacketed, the Other Always Seems Baggy

A Vatic-Scholastic Disputation upon Dave Snowden’s “Neither Baggy Nor Boxed” (1 November 2026) and Chris Mowles’s “Staying with the Trouble” (26 October 2026)

Nine Parts · Eighteen Questions · Three Rulings · Sources

At a Glance

This disputation examines two short essays from autumn 2026: Dave Snowden’s “Neither Baggy Nor Boxed” and Chris Mowles’s “Staying with the Trouble”, which it answered. Both concern how gatherings of people should be structured. The disputation tests both essays against a single predicate, follows that predicate’s roots through philosophy, classical Chinese thought and democratic theory, and ends with a practical discipline.

  1. The predicate. To the strait-jacketed, the other always seems baggy, and to the baggy, the other always seems boxed. We see other people’s constraints and are blind to our own.
  2. Snowden rightly shifts the question from how much structure to what kind, but wrongly places his own method outside the axis he describes (Part One).
  3. His animal roles borrow sacred symbols, and an appointed trickster protects the frame it pretends to challenge (Part Two).
  4. His case against open rooms tells as much against his own method as against the club (Part Three).
  5. Mowles rightly defends the human encounter, but his best examples, therapy and his own conference, depend on rigorous form (Part Four).
  6. Three traditions, three jackets: design, tradition, method-choice. None can be taken off (Part Five).
  7. Why we cannot see our own form: tacit knowing, the tool that withdraws from view, habit, thought collectives (Part Six). Classical China named the problem and located the answer in cultivation (Part Seven).
  8. Politics: who is in the room matters most; the frame must be contestable and power reversible (Part Eight).
  9. The proposal: a discipline of declared form, with three registers, six questions and three moments (Part Nine).

OvertureThe predicate and its lineage

In brief 3 min read
  • The predicate: those who have made their own constraints explicit tend to perceive other people’s tacit constraints as formlessness.
  • Henry James’s “loose baggy monsters” is the classic case: Tolstoy had a form that James could not see.
  • The twin predicate: to the baggy, the other always seems boxed. Snowden and Mowles each see clearly only the other’s garment.

Takeaway“Baggy” and “boxed” describe the observer’s position at least as much as they describe the room.

The predicate of this disputation is simple to state and difficult to escape: to the strait-jacketed, the other always seems baggy. Whoever has made their constraints explicit — codified, timed, rolled, named — will tend to perceive any practice whose constraints are tacit, sedimented or distributed as formless. Bagginess, on this account, is rarely a property of the thing observed. It is the shadow cast by the observer’s own buckles. The judgement “this has no form” very often means only “this has no form I recognise as form”.

The word itself carries this history. When Henry James, in the 1908 preface to The Tragic Muse, surveyed Thackeray’s The Newcomes, Dumas’ Les Trois Mousquetaires and Tolstoy’s War and Peace, he asked what such works — “large loose baggy monsters, with their queer elements of the accidental and the arbitrary” — could artistically mean. James was the supreme formalist of the Anglophone novel, a master of the controlled point of view, of the centre of consciousness held in a vice. From inside that discipline, Tolstoy looked shapeless. Yet Tolstoy had already answered, in his 1868 essay on the book, that War and Peace was neither novel nor epic nor chronicle but exactly what its author wished and was able to express, in the form in which it was expressed. A century of criticism has largely sided with Tolstoy: the book’s architecture — its oscillation between drawing-room and battlefield, its essayistic interruptions, its refusal of a single centre — is a form, merely not a Jamesian one. James’s “baggy” is the locus classicus of the predicate: the strait-jacket naming the other.

The essay under disputation takes this very adjective as its hinge. Dave Snowden, responding to Chris Mowles and, through Mowles, to Michael C. Jackson, proposes a third course between the “baggy” gathering and the “boxed” workshop. The present critique grants that much in his essay is right — and argues that its central architecture reproduces, with considerable sophistication, exactly the Jamesian error. It names as baggy what it cannot see as constrained; it names itself as “neither” from a position that is, inescapably, a jacket of its own.

No room has ever been without its constraints; the constraints one wears are precisely those one cannot see; and the serious question is therefore never whether a gathering is constrained, but by whom, to what end, and with what capacity on the part of its members to perceive what binds them.

And the predicate has a twin, which this disputation will not let either party forget: to the baggy, the other always seems boxed. Snowden reads Mowles’s gathering as loose; Mowles reads the facilitated workshop as a cage. Each describes the other’s garment with precision and his own with silence. The essays under disputation are therefore not a debate between structure and freedom. They are two men, each tailored to the bone, accusing each other of nakedness and of bondage at once.

First Interlude · A Declaration on Jackets

There has never been a room without walls. There are only rooms whose walls we raised ourselves, and can therefore see, and rooms whose walls were raised long before we entered, so that we pass them each day without perceiving that they are there. The facilitator inscribes his walls upon the flip chart, where they invite resentment because they are visible; the tradition inscribes its walls in the bodies and habits of its members — in when they speak and when they wait, in how they disagree and what they leave unsaid — where they invite no resentment because they present nothing to resent.

We have fallen into the habit of calling the first kind of wall structure and the second kind freedom, and both names are artefacts of position rather than descriptions of the world. Bagginess, properly understood, is what form looks like from the wrong side of the glass.

SynopsisThe text under disputation, fairly stated

In brief 2 min read
  • Mowles contrasted his loose research conference with a tightly facilitated workshop; Snowden replies to him, and through him to Jackson.
  • Snowden’s key move: the issue is not how much structure but what kind. Governing constraints fix outcomes; enabling constraints shape conditions.
  • His case against open rooms rests on three grounds: hidden power, entrainment on the first confident voice, and scale.
  • His alternative is the triopticon, with its Eagles, Ravens, Beavers, Coyotes and Magpies. In his postscript he concedes that he is no neutral judge of it.

Snowden’s essay, published on The Cynefin Co site on 1 November 2026 under a Goethe epigraph on mastery revealed through limitation, enters a three-way exchange. Mowles, on his Substack, had contrasted two events: the loose, conversational annual gathering of his research community, with little fixed beyond start and finish, and a tightly timed one-day facilitated workshop of flip charts, sticky notes and dot voting. Mowles had drawn on Sherry Turkle’s work on artificial intimacy and engaged Jackson’s critique of complex responsive processes. Snowden judges Mowles slightly unfair in aligning Jackson’s preference for structure with the workshop, but accepts the contrast for the sake of argument.

He agrees with Mowles against the workshop — its timed exercises, premature convergence, and what Doug Griffin called the doubling of process — and recalls his own experience of IBM’s trained methods for controlling groups. He shares Mowles’s worry, via Turkle, about passivity and cognitive outsourcing. But he relocates the question: not how much structure, but what kind. Governing constraints fix outcomes; enabling constraints shape conditions and make some things more likely without determining what emerges. Jazz, cooking and the martial-arts kata are offered as cases in which drilled form makes spontaneity possible.

Against the baggy gathering he argues that, absent structure, existing power — seniority, confidence, familiarity — structures the room; that groups entrain rapidly on the first confident voice; that a long-standing research community can afford open conversation because it is closer to a club; and that diverse settings — hospitals, ministries, communities in conflict — cannot, with the least powerful bearing the cost. An absent facilitator, he writes, does not abolish power: it “simply leaves it unexamined”.

His alternative is the triopticon: expert “Eagles” from different disciplines speak in turn; silent “Ravens” listen and then make sense in trios; trios recombine into “Beaver” groups to build concepts; outputs are clustered and owned; optional “Coyotes” challenge and “Magpies” carry ideas between groups; the convener stays out of the content. Roles cut across hierarchy, opposition is ritualised, odd numbers are said to avoid polarisation, premature consensus is designed out. The triopticon is presented as one assembly of method components governed by shared principles — distribute cognition, disrupt entrainment, ritualise conflict, disintermediate, work at the right granularity, avoid premature convergence — with an unnamed British hospital’s ritualised crew decision-making as a supporting illustration.

He closes by granting Mowles that slippage and frustration are where new thinking happens and Jackson that unstructured talk does not give the weakest a fair hearing, claims that anthro-complexity holds both, and proposes a joint test — perhaps in Hull — with Eagles from all three traditions. A postscript concedes that he may be reading a binary into Mowles that Mowles does not intend, that he is no neutral judge of his own method, that the triopticon costs time and can disorient, that a baggy gathering among trusting people achieves things a structured one cannot, and that choosing no constraints is still a choice.

Part OneThe disputation with Snowden: seven questions

Part One puts Snowden’s essay to the test in seven questions. Each follows the same four-step scholastic form, explained in the reading guide at the start: the case for his position, the case against it, the author’s answer, and replies to the original objections. Its verdict is set out in the Ruling that closes the Part.

In brief 18 min read
  • Question I — Mowles’s “baggy” conference is in fact densely constrained, by tradition and shared habit; a club is among the most structured of institutions.
  • Question II — Snowden’s two kinds of constraint (governing and enabling) leave out a third, the constitutive constraint that makes a community what it is. On that view the triopticon is a prosthetic for communities that lack such constitution.
  • Question III — Open rooms examine power reflexively, during and after the event; designed rooms do not remove power but move it upstream into the designer.
  • Question IV — Snowden’s jazz, kitchen and dojo analogies argue for long-incorporated tradition, not for a one-day role-script.
  • Question V — “Neither baggy nor boxed” claims a view from nowhere; trios, animal roles and ritualised conflict carry constraints of their own.
  • Question VI — Goethe’s sonnet, read whole, reconciles nature and art rather than mediating between them, and praises self-limitation.
  • Question VII — Testing the triopticon by running a triopticon is to judge one’s own case.

TakeawaySnowden is right that the kind of structure matters more than its amount, and that “making none is still a choice”. He is wrong to place his own method outside the axis he describes.

Question IWhether bagginess is a property of the gathering, or an attribution made from within a constraint regime

It may be urged bagginess is a property of the gathering. For Mowles himself describes his conference as having almost nothing fixed beyond its start and finish; Snowden merely adopts the self-description. Further, a thing may be measured by its explicit rules: where there is no agenda, no timing, no roles, there is by definition less structure. Further, Snowden’s whole argument concerns kinds of structure, and so cannot be accused of failing to see structure.

Yet against this stands Snowden’s own concession that the gathering is closer to a club — a body of people who share a tradition and its assumptions. A club is among the most densely constrained of human institutions. Its constraints are not printed on a programme because they are carried in its members: in a shared vocabulary (Elias, Mead, figuration, gesture-response), in a canon, in tacit norms of who may interrupt and how disagreement is voiced, in years of prior conversation that make each new remark legible. To call a club “baggy” is to confuse the absence of written rules with the absence of rules.

I answer that bagginess is a relational predicate disguised as a monadic one. It names the relation between an observer’s explicit constraint repertoire and a practice whose constraints are encoded otherwise. Bourdieu’s account of habitus — durable dispositions that structure practice without being consciously obeyed — and of doxa — that which goes without saying because it comes without saying — supplies the mechanism. Where constraint lives in habitus, it is invisible to outsiders and nearly invisible to insiders. It is perceptible chiefly at its edges: in the newcomer who does not know when to speak, the visitor who finds the long silences unnerving, the guest whose contribution “lands wrong”. Mowles, to his credit, reports exactly these dynamics of inclusion and exclusion. That report is itself evidence that the gathering is constrained — the constraints are what include and exclude.

Snowden half-sees this. His club remark registers that the conference has a form; but he treats the club’s form as a licence for openness (the club can afford it) rather than as the constraint that does the work. The difference matters. If the club’s shared tradition is what enables its open conversation to be productive, then the gathering is not an instance of minimal structure succeeding by luck among friends; it is an instance of maximal, sedimented, internalised constraint — precisely the condition that his own jazz and kata analogies celebrate (see Question IV). The baggy room is, on its own terms, the most constrained room in the essay.

Wittgenstein’s remarks on rule-following press the point further. A rule is not an object in the room; it shows itself in what practitioners take as going on in the same way. At bedrock, the spade is turned, and one says simply: this is what we do. To someone who requires every rule to be stated in advance, a practice that runs on bedrock agreement will look rule-less. It is not. Its rules simply cannot be read off a flip chart.

To the first objection that Mowles describes his gathering as loose does not settle the matter; insiders are poor witnesses to their own doxa, and the self-description is itself a move in the tradition’s self-understanding — complex responsive processes valorises the unplanned. To the second to measure structure by explicit rules is to define the question in the jacket’s favour; it is the Jamesian standard applied to Tolstoy. To the third Snowden does distinguish kinds of structure, but his taxonomy (see the next quaestio) has no comfortable place for the kind the club possesses, and so the club appears, by default, as a case of too little.

Question IIWhether the dyad of governing and enabling constraints suffices to classify the rooms in dispute

It may be urged it suffices. The distinction is clean, operational and borrowed from serious philosophy (Alicia Juarrero’s work on constraints in complex systems). It moves the debate from quantity to quality, which is a genuine advance on Mowles’s contrast.

Yet against this Juarrero’s own account does not stop at two. In Dynamics in Action (1999) and, more fully, Context Changes Everything (2023), the constraints that matter most for coherent wholes are those that constitute them — the context-dependent constraints by which interdependent parts come to form an entity with its own identity and its own capacity to act. A tradition, a community of practice, a club, a school of thought: these are constitutively constrained.

I answer that the governing/enabling dyad, as deployed in the essay, is a dyad of designer’s constraints. Both terms answer the question a facilitator asks before the event: shall I fix outcomes, or shape conditions? Neither answers the question a sociologist asks after the event: what made this group the group it was? The constitutive register is missing, and its absence is not innocent. The baggy gathering’s strength lies almost wholly in constitutive constraint; the triopticon’s strength lies almost wholly in enabling constraint introduced from outside. A taxonomy that lists only governing and enabling will therefore render the baggy room as a null case — neither governed nor enabled — and the triopticon as the paradigm of the good. The conclusion is pre-loaded into the categories.

Restoring the third term redraws the field. One can then say: the workshop imposes governing constraints on a group with weak constitutive constraint (strangers, or colleagues thrown together); the club conference relies on strong constitutive constraint and adds almost nothing; the triopticon imports enabling constraints designed to do, for a heterogeneous group in a day, part of what constitutive constraint does for a tradition over decades. On this reading the triopticon is not a midpoint between baggy and boxed. It is a prosthetic for missing constitution — valuable precisely where constitution is weak (the hospital, the ministry, the conflict community), and potentially redundant or even disruptive where it is strong.

That is a more modest and more defensible claim than the essay makes, and it is one the essay’s own scaling argument almost reaches. But it requires granting what the title denies: that the “baggy” room is not under-structured but differently structured.

To the first objection the distinction is indeed an advance, and nothing here disputes its value; the objection is that, in the essay’s use, it is incomplete in a way that systematically favours the author’s method. The move from quantity to quality is half made; the move from designed to constitutive quality is not made at all.

Question IIIWhether the unstructured room leaves power unexamined, and the structured room examines it

It may be urged the unstructured room leaves power unexamined. This is the classic finding of Jo Freeman’s “The Tyranny of Structurelessness” (written 1970, published 1972): groups that refuse formal structure do not escape structure but acquire informal elites, unaccountable precisely because unacknowledged. Snowden’s argument is Freeman’s argument transposed into facilitation.

Yet against this the tradition Snowden addresses is the one most explicitly organised around the examination of power. Complex responsive processes descends from Norbert Elias, for whom power is a structural characteristic of every human relationship, and the work of Stacey, Griffin, Shaw and Mowles has made the dynamics of inclusion and exclusion, of figuration and status, its central object. It would be strange if this tradition, of all traditions, left power unexamined.

I answer that the two parties examine power at different moments and in different modes, and the essay mistakes a difference of temporality for a difference of attention. Anthro-complexity examines power ex ante, by design: roles are distributed to cut across hierarchy, speaking turns are allotted, silence is imposed on Ravens to prevent entrainment. Complex responsive processes examines power in medias res and ex post, by reflexivity: the group notices who spoke, who fell silent, whose remark was taken up, and makes that noticing the material of further conversation. The latter is not an absence of examination; it is examination as the very substance of the event. The baggy room is, on its own understanding, a laboratory in which power is the specimen.

One may judge the reflexive mode insufficient — Snowden’s point about the least powerful bearing the cost is serious, and reflexivity after the fact is cold comfort to someone silenced during it. But the charge that power is unexamined is false to the tradition, and the essay’s one direct quotation from Mowles (that inclusion and exclusion play out all weekend) refutes it from Mowles’s own mouth.

Conversely, the designed room does not abolish unexamined power; it relocates it. Freeman’s remedy for structurelessness was not choreography but accountable structure: delegation by the group, distribution and rotation of authority, diffusion of information, structures the group itself can see, debate and change. The triopticon fails this test in an instructive way. Its roles, sequence, group sizes and permitted moves are authored elsewhere — in a field guide, by a method designer — and arrive on the day as given. The convener stays out of the content, but the content of a gathering is not only what is said; it is what may be said, by whom, in what order, in groups of what size. McLuhan’s medium-as-message applies with full force: the form is a content, and its author is fully present in the room though absent from the floor. Foucault would call this a dispositif: an arrangement of positions and visibilities that produces conduct without appearing to command it. The power has not been examined; it has been moved upstream, into the design, where participants cannot contest it without stepping outside the game.

The essay’s IBM anecdote sharpens the irony. Snowden recalls with distaste the training of staff in a corporate way of controlling groups. A branded method with named roles, published components, field guides and a wiki is not immune to the same routinisation; Weber’s routinisation of charisma describes exactly how a founder’s situated craft becomes an institution’s procedure. The question is not whether the triopticon is the IBM way — it is plainly more intelligent than that — but whether it carries within itself the means of its own contestation by the people subjected to it.

To the first objection Freeman is rightly invoked, but she proves more than the essay wants. She shows that every group is structured; she does not show that designed structure is accountable structure. Read whole, Freeman is as much a warning to the method designer as to the unconference.

Question IVWhether the analogies of jazz, kitchen and dojo testify for the triopticon

It may be urged they do. In each, drilled form precedes and enables freedom: the improviser’s years of repertoire, the cook who leaves the recipe only after long practice, the kata that makes improvisation in the street possible. So too the triopticon’s constraints enable emergent thought.

Yet against this in every one of these cases the constraint has been internalised over years by the practitioner, until it is no longer experienced as constraint at all. None of them describes a stranger handed a set of rules on the morning of a single event.

I answer that the analogies testify, but for the other side. Consider how a jazz session appears to a musician trained only in reading orchestral parts: no score, no conductor, players entering and dropping out by glance and gesture — baggy. Yet the session is governed by an immense, unwritten grammar of changes, standards, conventions of trading fours, signals for the head out. The freedom is the visible face of an invisible jacket worn so long it has become skin. The same is true of the cook’s departure from the recipe and of the fighter’s improvisation: each looks loose to the observer who sees only the moment, and each rests on constitutive constraint deposited in the body by repetition.

This is a description of the club conference, not of the triopticon. Mowles’s community, with its shared canon and years of conversation, is the jazz ensemble; its apparent bagginess is the improvisational freedom that sedimented tradition makes possible. The triopticon, by contrast, gives participants a novel role-script for a day. Its Ravens have not drilled silent listening for a decade; its Beavers have not practised recombination until it is second nature. In the terms of the analogies themselves, the triopticon stands closer to the beginner’s recipe card than to the master’s departure from it — closer, uncomfortably, to the workshop it opposes, if more cleverly designed.

One may rescue the analogy by a different route: the method’s designer is the master, the event is the performance, and participants are the instruments. But that rescue concedes the point of Question III, that freedom here is the designer’s and conduct is the participants’.

The hospital example fares no better as evidence. Ritualised, role-based crew decision-making in clinical settings is a well-established and genuinely valuable pattern; but in those settings the roles are trained repeatedly, embedded in professional identity and reinforced by consequence. They are kata. That such scaffolding works for a surgical team that rehearses it daily tells us little about whether a one-off assembly of strangers in animal roles will produce better thought than a long-standing community talking freely. The essay names no hospital, no study and no outcome.

To the first objection the inference from “drilled form enables freedom” to “this designed form enables this group’s freedom” requires the missing premise that the form has been drilled. Without it, the analogy argues that tradition is the condition of improvisation — which is Mowles’s case, not Snowden’s.

Question VWhether the triopticon is truly “neither baggy nor boxed”

It may be urged it is. It rejects governing constraints on outcome, as the baggy gathering does; and it rejects the absence of designed constraint, as the boxed workshop does. It sits, as the essay says, deliberately between conference and unconference.

Yet against this stands the converse of our predicate: to the baggy, the other always seems boxed. Present a practitioner of open, reflexive conversation with a room in which experts are designated Eagles, listeners are commanded to silence, groups are formed in trios by rule, recombined into Beavers by rule, challenged by appointed Coyotes and cross-pollinated by appointed Magpies — and the room will look not intermediate but heavily boxed. Indeed it may look more elaborately boxed than the sticky-note workshop, whose constraints at least are few and familiar.

I answer that “neither” is a claim to a view from nowhere, and the view from nowhere is not available in this dispute. The axis running from baggy to boxed is not a ruler laid on the world; it is a projection from each observer’s own jacket. Aristotle already knew this about means: in Nicomachean Ethics II.6 the mean is relative to us, not to the object, and what is the right ration for Milo the wrestler is too much for the novice. A “golden mean” between baggy and boxed is located by whoever draws the axis. Snowden draws it so that the workshop lies at one end, the club at the other, and his method in the centre. Mowles would draw it so that the club’s reflexive conversation sits at the healthy centre between authoritarian facilitation and anomie. Neither drawing is neutral. The essay’s title performs the oldest move in polemic — construct two extremes, then occupy the mean — and the postscript half-confesses it, conceding that the binary may be more Snowden’s construction than Mowles’s.

Three internal features of the method sharpen the point.

First, the trios. The essay asserts that odd numbers avoid polarisation. Georg Simmel’s classic analysis of the triad in his Soziologie (1908) argues something quite different: the step from two to three introduces the possibility of coalition, of the tertius gaudens who profits from the quarrel of the other two, and of divide et impera. Triads do not dissolve polarisation; they convert binary opposition into the structural isolation of a minority of one. Whether that is better or worse for the least powerful — the very constituency the essay invokes — is an empirical question the essay does not ask. The claim is offered as a design axiom; Simmel suggests it is at least contestable.

Second, the animals. Eagles, Ravens, Beavers, Coyotes, Magpies: the bestiary is charming and doubtless memorable. But Lévi-Strauss observed of totemic classification that animals are chosen less because they are good to eat than because they are good to think — they encode a social order in natural kinds. A role-system mapped onto species naturalises a design choice: it presents an authored division of labour as though it were a taxonomy of temperaments. That is precisely how jackets become invisible to those who wear them.

Third, ritualised conflict. Victor Turner’s work on ritual distinguishes structure from anti-structure: the liminal phase in which ordinary roles are suspended and communitas arises. Ritual can host anti-structure — but it does so by bounding it. Ritualised opposition is opposition domesticated; the Coyote challenges because it is the Coyote’s turn to challenge. Here the borrowed title of Mowles’s post becomes pointed: “staying with the trouble” is Donna Haraway’s phrase (Staying with the Trouble, 2016), and Haraway’s trouble is not a scheduled role but the unbidden mess of entangled lives that cannot be designed out or designed in. A method that designs where trouble may occur is not staying with the trouble; it is staging it. The essay concedes that slippage and frustration are where new thinking happens — and then proposes to manufacture slippage on cue.

There is, finally, the Griffin irony. Snowden enlists Doug Griffin’s criticism of the doubling of process against the workshop. But a method of named roles and phased recombinations is itself a second layer laid over conversation — a process about the process, foregrounding its own mechanics. From the complex-responsive-processes standpoint, the triopticon is doubling of process in a more refined form.

To the first objection the triopticon does reject governing constraints on outcome, and that is a real and important difference from the workshop. But rejecting one kind of box does not place a method outside the axis; it relocates it along an axis whose centre is a function of the drawer.

Question VIWhether Goethe’s sonnet serves the argument it heads

It may be urged it serves admirably. Its closing tercet is the classic German statement that mastery is revealed in limitation and that only law can give freedom:

Wer Großes will, muß sich zusammenraffen;
In der Beschränkung zeigt sich erst der Meister,
Und das Gesetz nur kann uns Freiheit geben.Who would do great things must gather himself; in limitation the master first shows himself, and only law can give us freedom.

Yet against this the sonnet opens with the counter-movement that the epigraph omits —

Natur und Kunst, sie scheinen sich zu fliehen
Und haben sich, eh man es denkt, gefunden;Nature and art seem to flee each other, and have found each other before one knows it.

I answer that the poem is a reconciliation, not a ranking. Its argument is that nature (the free, the unbidden, what James would call the accidental and the arbitrary) and art (form, law, limitation) only seem to oppose one another, and are found already joined. Read whole, the sonnet undoes the very binary that a “third way” requires; there is no need for a mediating method between baggy nature and boxed art, because the opposition was an appearance.

Further, the limitation Goethe praises is self-limitation: sich zusammenraffen, to gather oneself. It is the discipline the master takes upon himself in the pursuit of the great, not a role-script handed to a participant by a convener. The tercet thus sides once more with the constitutive constraint of the practitioner and the tradition (Questions II and IV) over the enabling constraint of the designer.

And there is a formal joke Goethe would have relished. The poem is a sonnet — the strait-jacket of European lyric — and Goethe was notably ambivalent about the form, which the Romantics around him were reviving with enthusiasm. The sonnet praising law is written by a poet who elsewhere chafed at this law. Mastery, for Goethe, was not choosing the right box but inhabiting a box so freely that its walls disappeared: the jacket become skin.

To the first objection the tercet is apt for the thesis that constraint and freedom are not opposed. It is not apt for the thesis that a designed intermediate method is superior to a tradition’s internalised form — and it is the latter thesis that the essay needs.

Question VIIWhether a joint trial “perhaps in Hull” could adjudicate between the traditions

It may be urged it could. The proposal is generous, empirical and in the spirit of safe-to-fail probing that anthro-complexity commends. Inviting Eagles from all three traditions is an act of intellectual hospitality.

Yet against this nemo iudex in causa sua — no one should be judge in their own cause. The proposed trial would test the triopticon by running the triopticon. Its constraints, roles and sequence would be those of one disputant.

I answer that the gesture is admirable and the design is circular. To ask whether a baggy conversation or a triopticon better serves thought by convening a triopticon is to adjudicate the dispute within the jacket of one party. A complex-responsive-processes practitioner invited as an Eagle would be required to speak in an allotted turn, then fall silent while Ravens made sense of her in trios, then watch Beavers build concepts from her remarks — every one of which is a constraint her tradition would contest. The Coyote role would be assigned to someone else. She could not do the thing her tradition does, which is to let the conversation find its own form and then reflect on what form it found. The trial would demonstrate, at most, that the triopticon is good at being a triopticon.

A fair test would require symmetry: a triopticon and an open reflexive gathering and perhaps a methodologically pluralist design of the kind Jackson’s critical systems thinking would propose (Jackson’s own commitment to coherent pluralism suggests he might regard the triopticon as one method among several, appropriate to some contexts, rather than as a reconciliation of traditions). Each would need its own criteria of success stated in advance — and here the deepest difficulty appears. The traditions do not agree on what a good gathering produces. Anthro-complexity values distributed cognition, disrupted entrainment and actionable outputs owned by participants. Complex responsive processes values the transformation of identity through conversation and the reflexive noticing of power, outputs that cannot be clustered on a wall. Critical systems thinking values emancipatory improvement and methodological fit. A trial without an agreed metric adjudicates nothing; a trial with one party’s metric adjudicates in that party’s favour.

The essay’s evidential posture compounds the problem. Its claims — that silent listening reduces early bias and broadens uptake, that odd numbers avoid polarisation, that premature consensus is designed out — are stated as design truths. None is supported by data in the essay; the field guides cited describe the method’s use rather than measure its effects; the proposed test is prospective. For a tradition that rightly prizes evidence over consultant folklore and safe-to-fail probes over grand design, this is a notable asymmetry: the baggy room is held to account for what it may do to the least powerful, while the triopticon’s benefits are asserted. The postscript’s candour — that Snowden is no neutral judge of his own method — is genuine and creditable. It should have been the essay’s premise, not its afterword.

A last, small observation. Hull is the historic seat of Jackson’s own school of critical systems thinking. To propose the trial there is courteous; it is also, perhaps, a reminder that every venue is a jacket too.

To the first objection the spirit of the proposal deserves full credit, and nothing here discourages it. What is disputed is the inference that such a trial, as sketched, could settle anything beyond the method’s internal coherence.

The RulingWhat stands, what falls, and the audit of jackets

In brief 4 min read
  • Stands: kind over quantity; the critique of sticky-dot workshops; power in open rooms is real; design earns its keep where trust is low.
  • Falls: the architecture of “neither”. If choosing no structure is a choice, then the “baggy” room is a design, not a void.
  • Proposed: an audit of jackets, in which every gathering declares its governing, enabling and constitutive constraints.

What stands

Much of the essay is right, and right in ways that matter. The relocation of the question from how much structure to what kind is a genuine advance over the conference-versus-workshop contrast. The critique of the sticky-note workshop — premature convergence, governing constraint masquerading as participation — is sound and widely shared. The warning that the absence of a facilitator does not abolish power is Freeman’s enduring insight, correctly applied. The insistence that diverse, high-stakes, low-trust settings cannot rely on the habits of a club is practically wise; it is in hospitals, ministries and divided communities that design earns its keep. The postscript is unusually honest for a method-owner, and its last concession — that choosing no constraints is still a choice — is the single most important sentence in the essay. It is also, followed through, the sentence that undoes the title.

What falls

What falls is the architecture of “neither”. If choosing none is a choice, then the baggy room is a design and not a void; then its constraints are real though unwritten; then calling it baggy is a perceptual report about the observer’s jacket, not a description of the room. The essay’s taxonomy omits the constitutive constraints in which the club’s form resides (Question II); its account of power mistakes a reflexive temporality for inattention and relocates the designer’s power upstream rather than examining it (Question III); its analogies testify for sedimented tradition rather than for a day’s role-script (Question IV); its claim to the mean is a claim to a view from nowhere, contestable by the converse predicate that to the baggy the other seems boxed (Question V); its epigraph, read whole, reconciles the binary it is enlisted to mediate (Question VI); its proposed adjudication is judged from within one party’s form (Question VII).

The predicate, restated

To the strait-jacketed, the other always seems baggy. The essay is a sophisticated instance of the rule, not an exception to it. Snowden’s jacket is beautifully cut — it fixes no outcomes, it ritualises dissent, it distributes voice — but it is a jacket, worn by a designer, and from inside it the club’s skin of tradition looks like no garment at all. The proper conclusion is not that the triopticon is bad (it may be excellent where constitution is weak), nor that the baggy room is innocent (Freeman stands), but that the dispute cannot be framed on a single axis of more and less structure without one party’s constraints becoming the measure of all.

The jacket audit

What would a symmetrical practice look like? It would require every gathering — baggy, boxed or between — to declare its constraints in three registers: the governing (what outcomes are fixed?), the enabling (what conditions are shaped, and by whom?), and the constitutive (what shared tradition, vocabulary, canon or habitus does this room depend on, and who lacks it?). The open conference would have to name its doxa — the unspoken canon that makes a newcomer’s remark land wrong. The triopticon would have to name its author — the designer present in every role and turn. Both would have to give participants the means, in Freeman’s sense, to see, debate and alter the jacket while wearing it. That is a harder discipline than either baggy freedom or designed scaffolding, and it belongs to neither tradition alone.

Coda in three registers: 道 · 理 · 勢

道 Dào, the Way: no gathering is wayless. The baggy room walks a way so worn it no longer looks like a path; the triopticon cuts a new path and posts signs at every fork. Both are ways; the error is to call the unsigned path wilderness.

理 Lǐ, pattern: the Shuowen glosses the character as the working of jade — the carver follows the veins already in the stone. Lǐ is constraint discovered in the grain, not imposed by a template. To the craftsman with a template, the carver who follows the veins seems to have no plan at all. That is the whole of our predicate in a single character. A method of complexity worthy of the name would read the grain of a group before laying a pattern upon it — and would sometimes find that the grain already holds the form.

勢 Shì, propensity: François Jullien’s reading of shì as efficacy arising from configuration rather than from imposed plan is closer to Snowden’s enabling constraints than anything in the Western canon he cites. Yet shì is the propensity of the situation, not of the method. The general who masters shì does not import a formation; he discerns which way things already incline. The baggy room and the boxed room each have their shì. The art is to discern it — and that art cannot be sealed in a field guide.

Bagginess is the name a jacket gives to another’s skin. Whoever calls a gathering formless discloses chiefly the contours of his own form; and the master, in Goethe’s sense, is not the one who has escaped limitation but the one in whom limitation, long and freely borne, has become indistinguishable from the body that bears it.

Part TwoThe borrowed bestiary: (post-)shamanic and (post-)Jungian symbology in the search for relevance

In brief 8 min read
  • Animal role-names are not neutral memory aids: they make an authored division of labour feel like a natural taxonomy (Lévi-Strauss).
  • Raven and Coyote come from specific Indigenous North American traditions, and reach management through Eliade, Harner and Jung.
  • An appointed trickster is a licensed jester: a “ritual of rebellion” that protects the frame it seems to challenge (Gluckman).
  • Either the symbols are decoration, which means borrowed aura, or they are meaningful, which means an unexamined metaphysics. Either way, symbolic power is power.

TakeawayA method that indicts unexamined power must examine the power of its own symbols.

The first part of this disputation treated the triopticon’s roles as constraints. This second part treats them as symbols. Eagle, Raven, Beaver, Coyote, Magpie: the method does not number its roles, nor name them functionally (speaker, listener, builder, challenger, carrier). It names them as animals, and two of those animals — Raven and Coyote — are among the most charged figures in the world’s mythologies: the tricksters of Indigenous North American narrative, and, after Paul Radin and C. G. Jung, the trickster archetype of Western depth psychology. The choice places the method, whether intended or not, within a recognisable register of contemporary organisational practice: the post-shamanic and post-Jungian idiom of councils, circles, talking pieces, totems, archetypal roles and “ancient wisdom”, through which facilitation seeks to clothe technique in enchantment. The question here is what that clothing claims, what it borrows, and what it costs.

A preliminary note of fairness. Snowden has often been a sharp and witty critic of New Age management — of colour-coded developmental schemes, of guru-led “presencing”, of facilitation that substitutes atmosphere for thought — and his methods claim grounding in cognitive science and anthropology rather than in mysticism. That is exactly why the bestiary merits scrutiny: it is the point at which a self-declared naturalist method borrows the aura of the very register it elsewhere mocks.

A symbol that goes unexamined orders a room as surely as a senior voice that goes unchallenged, and with this further advantage: that no one thinks to name it as a form of power at all.

Question VIIIWhether the bestiary is a mere mnemonic, or a symbolic claim

It may be urged it is a mnemonic only. Animal names are vivid, easy to remember, playful, and lower the solemnity of expert gatherings. No metaphysics is asserted by calling a listener a Raven; no one is asked to believe in Raven.

Yet against this if the names were merely mnemonic, any vivid set would serve — colours, chess pieces, planets, kitchen implements. The set chosen is not arbitrary. It assembles sky-seer (Eagle), trickster-knower (Raven), maker (Beaver), trickster-disruptor (Coyote) and thief-messenger (Magpie). It is a pantheon of functions drawn from the symbolic repertoire that Western neo-shamanism and Jungian psychology have made available as “universal”.

I answer that a name is never only a label: it carries a semantic field, and the field does work. Lévi-Strauss’s argument in Totemism (1962) is decisive here. Animals are chosen for totemic classification not because they are useful but because they are good to think: the differences between species are used to encode and naturalise differences between social groups. A role-system mapped onto species does exactly that. It converts an authored division of labour — who speaks, who must be silent, who builds, who disrupts — into something that feels like a taxonomy of natures. The Raven is not told “you are forbidden to speak now”; she is told she is a Raven, and Ravens listen. The constraint disappears into the totem.

Second, the repertoire is not ownerless. Raven is a culture-bearer and trickster in the narrative traditions of the Pacific Northwest Coast — Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Kwakwaka’wakw — embedded in clan structures, crests, protocols of who may tell which story and display which image. Coyote is the trickster of many Plains, Great Basin, Plateau and Californian traditions, again within specific ceremonial and seasonal rules (in some, Coyote stories are told only in winter). In those traditions the figures are not free-floating archetypes; they are kin, owned narratives and law. Their passage into management method runs through a specific Western mediation: Eliade’s construction of “shamanism” as a universal archaic technique (1951), Michael Harner’s “core shamanism” (1980), which deliberately abstracted practice from culture to make it portable, and the Jungian reading of tricksters as expressions of a collective unconscious common to all humanity. Ronald Hutton’s Shamans (2001) and Alice Kehoe’s Shamans and Religion (2000) have shown how much the universal category is a Western artefact; Philip J. Deloria’s Playing Indian (1998) traces the long Euro-American habit of borrowing Indigenous figures to resolve settler anxieties about authenticity and belonging.

The bestiary therefore is not neutral decoration. It is a post-shamanic gesture in the strict sense: it presupposes the prior Western extraction of shamanic figures from their peoples, and it inherits that extraction’s convenience — the figure without the obligations. The method that warns so carefully against unexamined power in the room has not examined the provenance of its own symbols.

To the first objection that no one is asked to believe in Raven does not make the name innocent. Symbols structure conduct without requiring belief; that is what Bourdieu meant by symbolic power — power that is exercised precisely because it is misrecognised as something other than power. If the names were truly empty, renaming the roles “Speaker, Listener, Builder, Challenger, Carrier” would cost nothing. The method’s practitioners would, one suspects, resist that renaming. The resistance is the measure of the claim.

Question IXWhether a trickster can be appointed

It may be urged it can. Organisations have long licensed dissent: the devil’s advocate (the advocatus diaboli of canonisation procedure), red teams, the court jester. A Coyote role simply gives the challenging function a name and a permission.

Yet against this in every tradition from which the figure is drawn, and in Jung’s own account, the trickster is defined by the fact that he is not licensed. He arrives uninvited, breaks the boundary, profanes the order, and is as likely to undo the convener as the participants.

I answer that an appointed trickster is a contradiction in terms, and the contradiction is instructive. Jung’s essay “On the Psychology of the Trickster Figure” (1954, published with Radin’s study of the Winnebago trickster cycle) reads the figure as the eruption of what consciousness has excluded — the shadow returning, uncontrolled. Lewis Hyde’s Trickster Makes This World (1998) reads him as the boundary-crosser whose disruptions create culture precisely because no existing order sanctions them. A trickster scheduled to challenge assumptions in the third phase, within a group of specified size, is neither Jung’s nor Hyde’s nor Coyote’s. He is the jester: licensed, bounded, and thereby made safe.

Anthropology has a precise name for this. Max Gluckman’s “Rituals of Rebellion in South-East Africa” (1954) showed that ritually permitted inversions — moments when subordinates may insult the king — tend to reinforce the established order by giving dissent a bounded channel. Bakhtin’s carnival has been read the same way by his sterner critics: sanctioned misrule as a safety valve. The ritualised opposition of the triopticon (Question V) and the appointed Coyote belong to this genus. They make challenge a role, and in doing so they relieve everyone else of the obligation, and remove from anyone the possibility, of a challenge that would overturn the frame itself. The one assumption a Coyote may not challenge is that there should be a Coyote.

The post-Jungian tradition sees this sharply. James Hillman’s archetypal psychology insisted that images are autonomous — that one does not use an archetype; one is used by it, and the attempt to harness it for ego purposes is the characteristic modern hubris. Andrew Samuels, in The Plural Psyche (1989), warned against archetypal language turning into a fixed typology that assigns persons to roles. On either account, archetypal role-assignment for the sake of workshop productivity is not post-Jungian depth; it is the instrumental flattening of the very thing depth psychology tried to protect.

To the first objection the devil’s advocate and the red team are honest about what they are: functional offices, named as such. The objection is not to licensed challenge, which is often valuable, but to dressing licensed challenge in the mantle of the unlicensed — borrowing the trickster’s aura of genuine disruption for an office whose whole design is to keep disruption in its place.

Question XWhether archetypal symbology confers relevance on method, or borrows it

It may be urged it confers relevance. Archetypal symbols speak to deep layers of human cognition; Jung argued that they recur across cultures because they express structures of the psyche. A method that uses them is more resonant, more memorable, more humanly engaging than one that speaks in bureaucratic functional language.

Yet against this the universality of archetypes is the most contested claim in Jung’s system. It is unfalsifiable in its strong form, and in its weak form (that certain images recur) it explains nothing that diffusion, shared ecology and shared narrative constraints cannot explain without positing a collective unconscious.

I answer that, in the market for methods, archetypal and shamanic symbology functions less as insight than as differentiation. Eric Abrahamson’s work on management fashion (1996) describes how techniques compete for attention through rhetorics that promise both rationality and progress; a second strategy, after Weber’s diagnosis of disenchantment, is to promise re-enchantment — to offer meaning, depth and ancient resonance in an organisational world drained of them. Hobsbawm and Ranger’s “invented tradition” names the move: novel practices acquire legitimacy by claiming continuity with a deep past. A facilitation method invented in the last two decades acquires, through its bestiary, the borrowed patina of the totem pole and the medicine story.

For anthro-complexity in particular this creates a strain that the essay does not acknowledge. The tradition presents itself as naturalistic: it appeals to cognitive science, to the evolutionary basis of narrative, to measured distributed cognition, and it has been severe on approaches it regards as pseudo-scientific. Either the animals are decorative — in which case the method is borrowing aura it has no right to, and the decoration should be dropped as the tradition would demand of anyone else — or they are meaningful — in which case the method imports, unexamined, a symbolic metaphysics (totemic, archetypal, shamanic) that its own epistemology would reject. There is no third option in which the symbols do work without making a claim.

And here the argument of Part One returns with force. The essay’s chief charge against the baggy room is that it leaves power unexamined. Symbolic power is power. A room whose roles are named for tricksters and sky-seers is structured by those names — by the expectations they carry of who sees from above (Eagle, the expert) and who must watch in silence (Raven), of who disrupts and who builds. To leave that symbolic structuring unexamined while indicting the club for its unexamined seniority is to commit, at the level of symbols, the very fault charged at the level of persons.

Finally, relevance. The search for relevance through enchanted symbols is a sign of a particular anxiety: that a technique, stated plainly, would not hold attention. But the strongest claim the triopticon can make — that structured sequencing of expert input, silent processing and small-group recombination can widen what a heterogeneous group considers — is a plain claim, testable in plain language. It needs no Raven. Its plain statement would be more honest and, for the sceptical audiences who matter most (clinicians, civil servants, the communities in conflict whom the essay invokes), probably more persuasive. Relevance borrowed from someone else’s sacred narrative is not relevance earned.

To the first objection resonance is real, and engaging language has value. But resonance drawn from a deracinated sacred repertoire carries debts — to the peoples whose figures they are, and to the truth about what the method actually is. A method of complexity should be the first to notice that symbols, like constraints, have provenance, and that provenance is part of meaning.

Part ThreeThe refutation: the passage on power, entrainment and scale, taken clause by clause

In brief 7 min read
  • Six claims are tested. Two of them (B and D) are true of every room and so discriminate nothing.
  • Two (A and C) tell against the triopticon: putting experts first formalises recognised power and engineers an anchor.
  • Claim E rests on an output metric and a reversible “club” charge; the Macy Conferences and Bohm’s dialogue complicate it.
  • Claim F, once its slip is corrected, names the out-group homogeneity effect: the outsider’s bias, which undermines the club charge itself.

The passage under refutation is the essay’s central polemical paragraph-set: three short movements on power, entrainment and scale, each directed at the unstructured gathering. Its claims are here set out in paraphrase, lettered for reference, and each is answered in the scholastic manner. The refutation does not deny that power, entrainment and scale are real; it denies that they discriminate between the unstructured room and the designed one in the way the passage requires — and it shows that, at several points, the designed room fares worse by the passage’s own criteria.

Claim A — In the absence of structure, recognised power structures the room: seniority, confidence, familiarity and the regulars’ habits decide who speaks, who is heard, and which ideas survive.

Reply. First, the premise “nothing is structured” describes no actual room. As Part One argued, the gathering in question is constitutively structured by a shared tradition; the antecedent of the conditional is never satisfied, so the conditional proves nothing about real gatherings.

Second, the consequent is not avoided by design. Status characteristics theory (Berger, Cohen and Zelditch, from the 1970s onward) shows that diffuse status cues — seniority, profession, gender, accent, perceived expertise — shape whose contributions are sought, credited and adopted regardless of formal role allocation, unless the structure actively works to disconfirm them. Formal roles do not dissolve these expectations; they are read through them.

Third — and decisively — the triopticon does not merely fail to neutralise recognised power; it formalises it. Its opening phase gives the floor to designated experts from established disciplines, while everyone else listens in enforced silence. That is the lecture: the oldest structure of recognised authority in the academy. Seniority and expertise, which the passage complains informally decide who speaks, here decide it by rule. The method does not examine recognised power; it appoints it, and calls it Eagle.

Claim B — A facilitator’s absence no more removes power than a facilitator’s claim to neutrality does; it leaves power unexamined.

Reply. The symmetry is correct, and it cuts against the method. If a facilitator’s neutrality is a fiction, so is the claim that the convener “stays out of the content”. The convener — and behind the convener, the designer — is in the form, and the form is a content (Question III). The passage thus states the principle by which its own method is indicted: a designer’s self-effacement is a claim to neutrality, and like any such claim it leaves the designer’s power unexamined. As for the unstructured room, Part One showed that its tradition examines power reflexively, in and after the event; “unexamined” is false of the tradition under discussion, and the essay’s own report of Mowles’s attention to inclusion and exclusion confirms it.

Claim C — Groups entrain quickly on familiar patterns, and the first confident voice shapes what follows.

Reply. Granted as a general finding; denied as a discriminator. The best-known evidence on this point — Tversky and Kahneman on anchoring (1974), Asch on conformity, the brainstorming literature summarised by Diehl and Stroebe (1987) on production blocking — supports a specific remedy: independent thought before exposure to others’ views (the logic of the nominal group technique and of Delphi). The triopticon reverses this order. It exposes everyone first to a sequence of confident, credentialed voices, then imposes silence while the group absorbs them, then asks trios to make sense of what they have heard. That is not the disruption of entrainment; it is the engineering of an anchor. The first confident voices are chosen in advance and given the floor by rule. Diversity among Eagles widens the set of anchors, which is a real benefit, but it does not escape the anchoring structure; it multiplies it.

Claim D — A conversation free of structure is not free of pattern; it is governed by the patterns people brought with them.

Reply. True of every conversation, and therefore probative against none. Participants carry their patterns into trios and Beaver groups as surely as into an open plenary; roles do not strip habitus at the door. The sentence is, in fact, the most important concession in the passage, because it admits that patterns brought in — tradition, habitus, constitutive constraint — govern rooms. That is the thesis of this disputation: the “unstructured” room is structured by what its members carry. The question then becomes whether the patterns brought in are generative or stultifying; and for a long-standing community with a shared, reflexive tradition, the essay’s own jazz analogy (Question IV) says they may be the very condition of improvisation.

Claim E — A research group’s weekend, among people who know and trust each other, can afford to let things go nowhere; it is closer to a club; open conversation among the like-minded differs from bringing together disciplines that disagree.

Reply. Three replies. First, “going nowhere” presupposes an output metric — clusters, owners, actions — which is one tradition’s criterion of value, not a neutral one (Question VII). For a tradition that locates the effect of conversation in the transformation of participants’ thinking and identity, nowhere is not where such a weekend goes.

Second, the “club” charge is a tu quoque waiting to happen. Every school of practice is a club in this sense, anthro-complexity included, with its own language (Cynefin domains, enabling constraints, safe-to-fail probes), its own canon and its own regulars. That a tradition is shared is not a vice; it is the condition of any cumulative inquiry.

Third, it is simply not the case that open conversation across disagreeing disciplines must fail. The Macy Conferences on cybernetics (1946–53) brought together mathematicians, neurophysiologists, anthropologists and psychiatrists in loosely structured discussion and helped found a field — while also, as their historians note, displaying exactly the status dynamics the passage describes. David Bohm’s dialogue practice was designed for sustained disagreement with minimal procedural structure, relying instead on the suspension of assumptions. Both cases support a mixed verdict: open conversation across difference can be generative and status-ridden. That is precisely the verdict the passage’s binary prevents it from reaching.

Claim F — Groups that, from outside, seem homogeneous in their assumptions are perceived within the group as significantly homogeneous.

Reply. As written in the passage supplied, this sentence asserts that groups seen as homogeneous from outside are seen as homogeneous from inside — which contains no contrast, and so cannot be “curious”. The context strongly suggests the intended word was heterogeneous: groups that look uniform to outsiders appear internally diverse to their members. If so, the sentence names a well-documented phenomenon — the out-group homogeneity effect (Quattrone and Jones, 1980; Linville and colleagues, 1989): we perceive out-groups as more uniform than in-groups, because we know our own group’s internal differences and lack that knowledge of others.

And there the passage refutes itself. The out-group homogeneity effect is a bias of the outside observer. It predicts that Snowden, observing the complex-responsive-processes community from outside, will perceive it as a homogeneous club, while its members perceive significant internal disagreement. The effect does not show that the club is homogeneous; it shows that outsiders will think so whether or not it is. The passage’s “curious” observation is, properly understood, the empirical form of this disputation’s predicate: to the strait-jacketed, the other always seems baggy — and, we may now add, to the outsider, the other always seems of one mind. In citing the effect, the essay supplies the very reason to distrust its own characterisation of the club.

What the refutation concludes

Taken together, the six claims do not establish that the unstructured gathering is peculiarly exposed to power, entrainment and parochialism. Claims B and D are true of every room and so discriminate nothing. Claims A and C, examined against the triopticon’s own design, tell more strongly against the method than against the club: it formalises recognised expertise and engineers an anchor. Claim E rests on a contestable metric and a reversible charge, and history supplies counter-instances. Claim F, once its evident slip is corrected, names the very bias that discredits the outsider’s description of the club. What survives is a modest and true proposition — that heterogeneous, low-trust, high-stakes groups benefit from carefully designed enabling constraints that are visible, contestable and accountable to those who wear them — which is the jacket audit proposed in the Ruling, and which belongs to neither party alone.

What the outsider dismisses as a club, the member experiences as an argument conducted over decades; what the designer offers as scaffolding, the participant may experience as bars; and since every gathering is clothed in some form, the most honest thing a gathering can do is declare who made its garment and on what authority.

Part FourThe trouble with staying: an interrogation of Chris Mowles’s “Staying with the Trouble”

In brief 21 min read
  • Mowles’s chain from chatbot to workshop to Jackson convicts by family likeness. The real danger, frictionlessness, points at the like-minded club.
  • His conference is a liturgy, resting on the rigorous frame of group analysis, which the essay never declares.
  • Therapy, his best example, is the most tightly framed encounter there is: a crack needs a vessel.
  • Rosa, Han and Jaeggi, read in full, support the idea of enabling conditions rather than a rejection of design.
  • Jackson’s question about who gets heard goes unanswered, and “going nowhere” is a privilege, Bourdieu’s skholè.
  • He felt the trouble in the workshop and complied. His title is Haraway’s, and Haraway rejects both the jeremiad and the romance of a world without machines.

TakeawayMowles’s enemy is not structure but alien structure: a form that has not yet become his skin.

Snowden’s essay was a reply. The first three parts of this disputation weighed the reply; justice now requires weighing what it replied to. Chris Mowles — Professor of Complexity and Management at Hertfordshire, director of its Doctor of Management programme, associate member of the Institute for Group Analysis — published “Staying with the trouble”, subtitled On working with uncertainty, on his Substack On complexity on 26 October 2026. It is a reflective essay rather than a study — personal observation braided with secondary authorities, written in a humane and melancholy key — and that is no fault in itself. But an essay that indicts structure as the enemy of the living encounter must itself be read for its structure, and an essay that champions the uncertain must be asked what it is certain of.

The essay, fairly stated

Mowles opens with Sherry Turkle’s Artificial Intimacy (2026): AI does not only learn from us but trains us, making us more passive, dependent and averse to experience. A machine that never argues back is easier than a flawed human, and the ease forecloses surprise. Through Hartmut Rosa he sets against this the idea of resonance — the mutual capacity to affect and be affected. Turkle names a passage from an attention economy to an attachment economy, in which people confide their inner lives to bots. Mowles calls the bot a thin respondent without hinterland, with no stake in what it advises, offering a probabilistic reworking of scraped text.

Turkle recounts Eric Schmidt’s view that AI will end the therapist’s trade, since psychological knowledge is now on tap. Mowles answers that this confuses knowledge with wisdom and phronetic judgement and misunderstands therapy, which turns on an alliance with a feeling human body that wants the client’s good — an alliance whose messy spontaneity is where, borrowing a famous line of Leonard Cohen’s, the light gets in. He grants that some therapists are poor or ill-motivated.

AI is then set within a larger diagnosis: individualism, competition and instrumentalisation (Rosa; Byung-Chul Han’s Burnout Society); the acceleration of Putnam’s Bowling Alone; a steep rise in loneliness, isolation and anxiety, especially among the young (citing a House of Lords Library briefing); and, through Arendt, the link between mass loneliness and the conditions of totalitarianism. We are, he concludes, destroying the space for shared experience. His own university supplies the local case: colleagues and students avoiding face-to-face contact, humanities closing, the particular enacting the general. Where, he asks, can the spontaneous, the novel and the eccentric now live?

Then the two events. His research group’s annual conference keeps a steady form — Friday supper, a Saturday keynote, then open conversation, fixed start and finish, presentation minimised. It is baggy, sometimes conflictual, marked by inclusion and exclusion, yet often the highlight of the delegates’ year, not least because the group can talk about how it is talking. He recalls his late colleague Doug Griffin’s phrase, the doubling of process — the felt need for a process in order to have a process. The facilitated strategy workshop, by contrast, was tightly timed and constantly reorganised; flip-chart outputs were read aloud and applauded; votes were cast with sticky labels; he felt pushed from pillar to post, met no one, reflected on nothing, and went along with it like a drone. He insists the lesson is not conference good, workshop bad; each serves a function. His objection is to the assumption that meaningful discussion needs total structure, which squeezes out the uncertain and the capacity to affect and be affected.

Finally Jackson. In Critical Systems Thinking and the Management of Complexity (2019) Jackson compares Stacey’s complex responsive processes unfavourably with soft systems thinking, frustrated that Stacey privileges conversation over schematic method, and asks whether it serves critical systems thinking’s commitment to democratic participation and the protection of disadvantaged stakeholders. On Mowles’s reading Jackson prefers more structure because badly chaired meetings go nowhere, and treats open, shared sense-making as such a meeting. Mowles replies, in a footnote, that badly chaired meetings are those that race through the agenda with no time to think. In the body he answers that going nowhere can be the point: it yields material for noticing; if our problems are second-order — political and social rather than technical, as Rahel Jaeggi argues — then how we handle dilemmas together is itself the curriculum. What is needed is transformation in how we address things, not more methods that pre-shape talk. He closes by asking whether our anti-uncertainty instruments, from AI to schematic tools, diminish our capacity to become ourselves, as they seek to “cabin, crib and confine” what is most valuable in experience.

Second Interlude · On the Mourning of Rooms

Every elegy for the open room is composed in a closed form. Mowles laments the passing of the spontaneous in ordered paragraphs, under numbered footnotes, in a sequence of his own choosing — and he could do no other, for a lament that refused all form would not be read, and a lament that is not read consoles no one.

This is not hypocrisy, and it is not offered here as a reproach. It is the condition under which any of us speaks of freedom at all: we can only describe the unbounded in a form that holds still long enough to be understood. That condition is, in miniature, the whole of the present argument.

Question XIWhether the chain from chatbot to workshop to Jackson holds

It may be urged that it holds. The essay identifies a single tendency — the flight from uncertainty — and shows it at three scales: the individual confiding in a machine, the group processed by a facilitator, the discipline preferring schematic method. To see one pattern across scales is precisely the gift of complexity thinking; the essay itself says the particular plays out in the general.

Yet against this a pattern seen across scales is only as good as the resemblance at each of its joints, and between the chatbot and the workshop, and between the workshop and Jackson, the joints are merely analogical — and analogy is the form of argument that convicts by family likeness.

I answer that the essay’s argument is built as a chain of contagion. Its first link is strong: Turkle’s chatbot is a respondent with no body, no stake, no hinterland. Its second link transfers that charge to a room full of embodied people with stakes and hinterlands, constrained by a bad design. Its third link transfers it again to a scholar who argued, on emancipatory grounds, for some form in deliberation. At each step the moral colour of the first link — the hollow, flattering machine — seeps forward. By the end, structure as such stands faintly accused of artificial intimacy. Jackson, whose concern is the voice of the disadvantaged, finds himself on the side of Eric Schmidt.

Snowden saw this and named it: framed this way, anyone who prefers structure ends up on the drone’s side. Mowles disclaims the binary and enacts it, for the essay is not a ledger in which both columns are kept. The conference is narrated from within, with affection, as the year’s highlight; the workshop is narrated from within, with distaste, as an ordeal. One instance of each; the best of the open form against what is very nearly the worst of the structured. The sticky-dot workshop is to structure what the pub argument is to conversation. A fair comparison would set the best open gathering against the best designed one — a well-run citizens’ assembly, a Quaker meeting for business, a clinical morbidity and mortality conference, a well-held group-analytic session. The essay does not make that comparison, and its rhetorical victory depends on not making it.

There is a deeper category error at the first joint. Turkle’s complaint about the bot is not that it is structured but that it is without friction: it never pushes back, never wants anything, never refuses. By that criterion the danger is not structure; it is the absence of resistance. And the room most at risk of frictionlessness is not the workshop — whose constraints chafe, as Mowles testifies — but the gathering of the like-minded, where tradition supplies agreement before anyone speaks. The club is the human form of the flattering machine. It too can become an attachment economy: one returns each year for the warmth of being understood. The essay’s own first link, held firmly, points at its own beloved room.

To the first objection seeing pattern across scales is indeed the gift of complexity thinking; mistaking resemblance for identity is its besetting vice. The particular plays out in the general only where the mechanism is shared. Here the mechanism differs at every joint — machine frictionlessness, managerial over-design, a methodological argument about deliberative fairness — and what is shared is the author’s mood.

Question XIIWhether the conference is baggy by its own description

It may be urged that it is: almost nothing is fixed but the start and the finish; presentation is minimised; the conversation goes where it goes.

Yet against this read Mowles’s own description slowly — supper on the Friday, a keynote on the Saturday, open conversation thereafter, fixed hours at either end, and the same order faithfully repeated year upon year — and it becomes plain that what is described is not the absence of form but a liturgy.

I answer that the conference as Mowles describes it is a ritual calendar of remarkable stability: a communal meal that opens the rite, a single authorised voice that sets the theme (the keynote is the sermon), then a long period of open exchange within boundaries of time and membership, repeated annually. Anthropology would recognise the shape at once: commensality, proclamation, assembly. Its constraints are few but heavy, and they are constitutive (Question II) — they make the community what it is.

Behind the liturgy stands a lineage that the essay never names but everywhere relies on. Mowles is an associate member of the Institute for Group Analysis, and the gathering’s prized capacity — to talk about how it is talking — is the signature competence of group analysis. Yet S. H. Foulkes’s group analysis is among the most carefully structured of all human gatherings: a conductor, a fixed membership, a fixed time and place, a circle, a set of norms about free-floating discussion, and what Foulkes called dynamic administration — the conductor’s continuous tending of the boundaries. The famous freedom of the group-analytic matrix is purchased by a rigorous frame. The research conference inherits that freedom and quietly inherits the frame, but tells itself the story of the freedom only.

Here the twin predicate bites. Mowles, tailored by decades of group-analytic and complex-responsive practice, perceives his own garment as bagginess and the facilitator’s as a straitjacket. But reflexive talk about talk is not the absence of process; it is a second-order process, governed by tacit norms of what counts as noticing, who may interpret whom, and when an interpretation lands. Griffin’s doubling of process — the need for a process in order to have a process — returns upon the conference that quotes it: a group that must talk about how it talks in order to talk well has also built a process on top of its process. The doubling is not eliminated; it is internalised and renamed reflexivity.

The group-analytic and psychoanalytic lineage on which the essay silently draws also supplies the strongest warrant for Snowden’s charge of entrainment — a warrant the essay never confronts. W. R. Bion’s Experiences in Groups (1961) distinguished the work group, oriented to its task, from the basic-assumption group, which falls into shared unconscious postures — dependency on a leader, fight-or-flight, or pairing in the hope of a messiah to come. Bion’s finding was that relatively unstructured groups slide readily into basic-assumption mentality; the first confident voice, in his idiom, is the voice the group appoints to carry its dependency. Isabel Menzies Lyth’s study of nursing (1960) adds the other edge: organisational structures often function as social defences against anxiety, protecting members from the raw demands of the work at the cost of its meaning. Menzies Lyth arms Mowles against the workshop — its timetable and applause may be a defence against the anxiety of not knowing. But she arms Snowden against the conference too: a cherished annual liturgy, among those who already understand one another, may be the subtlest defence of all, a defence against the anxiety of the stranger. It is striking that an essay subtitled On working with uncertainty, from a tradition steeped in group analysis, mentions anxiety only as a population statistic and never as a dynamic in its own rooms.

To the first objection minimal programme is not minimal structure. The fewer the rules on paper, the more must be carried in the members, and the higher the price of entry for anyone who does not already carry them. That is why the conference produces, by Mowles’s own account, dynamics of inclusion and exclusion. Those dynamics are what the invisible jacket feels like to someone not yet fitted for it.

Question XIIIWhether the example of therapy testifies against structure

It may be urged that it does. Therapy is the paradigm of the irreducibly human encounter: the alliance of two embodied persons, spontaneous and messy, where wisdom and not information heals. AI and manualised method alike threaten it.

Yet against this no human encounter is more rigorously framed than the therapeutic hour.

I answer that the essay has chosen as its emblem of unscripted encounter the single practice in which constraint is most explicit, most theorised and most defended. Freud’s papers on technique fixed the hour, the fee, the regularity, the couch, the rule of free association and the analyst’s abstinence. Winnicott called the setting a holding environment and insisted that its reliability is what allows the patient to risk chaos within it. Robert Langs built a whole clinical theory on the integrity of the frame, arguing that breaches of the frame are among the most potent events in treatment. Group analysis, as noted, adds dynamic administration. The spontaneity Mowles rightly treasures is not the opposite of this structure; it is its fruit. The messiness is safe to enter precisely because the walls are reliable.

And here the Cohen allusion turns. A crack is a property of a vessel. Where there is no vessel there is no crack, only spillage. The light comes in through the flaw in the form — and therefore needs the form. Mowles has reached, without intending it, Goethe’s reconciliation (Question VI): nature and art have found each other before one knows it. His example is the strongest argument in the essay, and it is an argument for the right structure, not against structure.

The point matters for his quarrel with Schmidt as well. What Schmidt misunderstands is not merely the difference between knowledge and wisdom, though that is real; it is the frame. A chatbot is available at every hour, without fee, without ending, without abstinence, without the discipline of the fifty minutes. It is frameless. The danger Turkle describes is not too much structure in the encounter but too little: boundless availability, infinite patience, no ending. By Mowles’s own best example, the bot is not a straitjacket. It is a room with no walls — and it is the wall-less room that makes us passive.

To the first objection therapy is indeed the paradigm of the human encounter, and for that very reason it refutes the essay’s drift. It teaches that the deepest spontaneity flourishes inside the most reliable form, and that the enemy of resonance is the formless and frictionless, not the framed.

Question XIVWhether resonance can do without conditions

It may be urged that resonance, in Rosa’s sense, is precisely what cannot be engineered. It is a two-way responsiveness that arises unbidden; the attempt to produce it destroys it. Methods that seek to secure good conversation therefore kill what they seek.

Yet against this Rosa does not say that resonance arises from nowhere; he says that it cannot be commanded, which is a different claim, and that it depends upon conditions which can be cultivated or destroyed.

I answer that the essay enlists Rosa for half of his thesis. In The Uncontrollability of the World Rosa argues that resonance is uncontrollable in the sense that it cannot be produced at will or accumulated, but that it is not random either: it requires what he elsewhere calls axes of resonance, institutional and material settings that make responsiveness possible without guaranteeing it. One might say resonance is half-available — beyond command, but not beyond cultivation. A concert hall does not produce the moment when the music takes the audience; but a hall with dead acoustics forbids it. The question for any gathering is therefore not whether to have conditions but which conditions leave the most room for what cannot be commanded.

That is, almost word for word, Snowden’s distinction between governing constraints, which fix outcomes, and enabling constraints, which shape conditions without determining what emerges. Mowles’s own authority, read whole, stands nearer to Snowden than to the essay that cites him. The quarrel between the two men shrinks, under Rosa, from a dispute about structure to a dispute about acoustics.

Han’s diagnosis cuts the same way. The Burnout Society argues that late-modern subjects are exhausted not by prohibition but by unlimited permission — the achievement-subject exploits itself because nothing stops it. Han’s pathology is too few walls, not too many. To cite Han against structure is to cite the physician of formlessness against form.

To the first objection what cannot be engineered can still be hosted. The host does not compel the guest’s delight; the host sets the table. To refuse to set the table for fear of compelling delight is not reverence for the uncontrollable. It is abdication dressed as reverence.

Question XVWhether Jackson has been answered

It may be urged that he has. Jackson thinks open conversation is a badly chaired meeting; Mowles shows that the badly chaired meeting is in fact the over-driven one, racing through its agenda with no time to think. The charge is returned to sender.

Yet against this by Mowles’s own account, Jackson’s question was not about chairing. It was about who gets heard — democratic participation and the protection of disadvantaged stakeholders.

I answer that the essay converts an ethical-political objection into a methodological-temporal one and then answers the conversion. Jackson asks: when sense-making is left to open conversation, who is protected? Mowles answers: the hurried meeting leaves no time to think. Both statements may be true, and they do not touch. The disadvantaged stakeholder is not protected by slowness; a slow room can silence as surely as a fast one, and more comfortably. The footnote’s quip about all activity and no thinking is witty and beside the point.

Worse, the essay grants the premise of Jackson’s question without noticing. It reports that the beloved conference has dynamics of inclusion and exclusion. That is the phenomenon Jackson is asking about. A complex responsive processes reply exists — that power relations are always present, that the work is to notice and negotiate them rather than design them away, that design merely hides the designer’s power (as this disputation argued against Snowden in Question III). But the essay does not make that reply. It leaves Jackson’s emancipatory concern standing, unanswered, at the edge of the conference, like the newcomer who does not yet know when to speak.

There is also a question of fidelity. Jackson’s position reaches the reader through Mowles’s paraphrase and a single page reference, and reaches Snowden’s readers through Mowles’s paraphrase again. Snowden says his own reading of Jackson differs. Jackson thus appears in this exchange as a ventriloquised third party — a figure about whom two traditions disagree, voiced by neither of them in his own words. A discussion of whose voice is heard in a room has, in its own textual room, a participant who has not yet spoken.

To the first objection the charge was not returned to sender; it was redirected to a different address. Jackson’s letter is still unopened.

Question XVIWhether “going nowhere” is a virtue or a privilege

It may be urged that it is a virtue. If our problems are second-order — crises in our forms of life, not technical puzzles — then the experience of how we handle dilemmas together is itself the material of learning. Going nowhere generates the data for noticing. The gaps and frustrations are where new thoughts become thinkable.

Yet against this to be able to go nowhere is to be free of the need to arrive, and that freedom, like most freedoms, is very unevenly distributed.

I answer that the essay describes, with honesty and tenderness, a luxury good and calls it a method. Bourdieu, in Pascalian Meditations, named the condition: skholè, the leisure from necessity that underlies the scholastic point of view, and the scholastic fallacy of mistaking the perspective afforded by that leisure for the human perspective as such. A weekend in which nothing need be decided is available to tenured scholars, doctoral candidates with institutional support, consultants between engagements. It is not available to the ward sister who must agree a rota by Monday, the civil servant whose minister needs a recommendation, the community whose dispute will turn violent if unresolved. To these, going nowhere is not a curriculum. It is abandonment.

Snowden’s scaling argument (which this disputation has otherwise contested) is here unanswerable on Mowles’s own terms. The essay’s examples of where spontaneity should live — the university, the research group — are sites of comparative leisure. Its example of where structure oppresses — the strategy workshop — is a site of decision. The comparison smuggles a difference of situation into a difference of method.

And Jaeggi, whom the essay invokes, does not license the virtue it claims. Her account of progress and regress treats forms of life as attempts to solve problems and judges them by whether they enable or block learning. In that idiom, going nowhere is neither good nor bad in itself; it is progressive when it opens a blocked learning process and regressive when it merely repeats. A tradition that returns each year to talk about how it talks, among people who already share its language, runs a real risk of the second. Second-order problems demand second-order learning — and a second-order learning process can be as stuck as a first-order agenda.

To the first objection that frustration may birth new thought is true. That it reliably does so, for whom, and at whose cost, the essay does not ask. A virtue that only the secure can afford is a virtue of the secure; it should be practised with the humility of knowing so.

Question XVIIWhether the drone stayed with the trouble

It may be urged that the confession of drone-like compliance is the essay’s most honest moment: it reveals how total structure produces passivity even in a seasoned critic. The workshop made Mowles a drone; that is its indictment.

Yet against this the trouble was in the room — Mowles felt it plainly as frustration, alienation and the ache of having no space to reflect — and by the essay’s own doctrine such frustration is exactly where new thinking becomes possible; yet he went along with it.

I answer that the confession indicts the confessor at least as much as the workshop. Staying with the trouble, in that room, would have meant voicing the dissonance there: saying aloud, in the plenary or to the facilitator or to a neighbour, that the process was preventing thought; refusing a sticky dot; asking the group to notice how it was being moved. Those are exactly the moves of a practitioner trained to talk about how we are talking. They were available. Constraint did not abolish them; it raised their cost. Mowles chose — as most of us would — to pay the lesser cost of compliance in the room and to recover his agency later, in prose, among his readers.

That is entirely human. But it reveals that the essay’s disposition to stay with trouble is selective. It stays with the trouble of the beloved conference, where frustration is honoured as fertile. It flees the trouble of the alien workshop, where frustration is treated as proof of oppression. The difference between fertile trouble and oppressive trouble turns out to be the difference between one’s own tradition and another’s. That is the predicate again, in its affective register: to the baggy, the other’s frustration seems like a cage; one’s own seems like growth.

Shakespeare supplies the deepest gloss, and the essay supplies the cue. Its closing triad — cabin, crib, confine — echoes Macbeth on hearing that Fleance has escaped: “But now I am cabin’d, cribb’d, confin’d, bound in / To saucy doubts and fears.” Consider who speaks. Macbeth is the man who tried to abolish uncertainty by violence — to make the prophecy safe by murdering the future. He is confined not by any structure imposed on him but by his own fear, the moment his control fails. The great literary image of being cabined and cribbed is an image of interior imprisonment: the jacket stitched from one’s own anxiety. Read through its source, the essay’s final figure says something its author may not have meant: that confinement is less often done to us by method than by the dread with which we meet what we cannot control — a dread from which no tradition, open or structured, is exempt.

To the first objection the confession is indeed honest, and honesty deserves honour. But honesty about passivity is not evidence that structure caused it. Group analysis itself knows that the group which goes along is enacting something, and that the enactment belongs to all its members — the drone included.

Question XVIIIWhether the essay keeps faith with its borrowed title

It may be urged that it does. To stay with the trouble is to remain present to uncertainty instead of fleeing into technique; the essay counsels just that, against AI and schematic method alike.

Yet against this the phrase is Donna Haraway’s, the title of her 2016 book, and the essay never names her — an omission that matters more than a missing citation ordinarily would, because Haraway’s trouble is not Mowles’s.

I answer that the title borrows an aura it does not repay. Haraway’s Staying with the Trouble: Making Kin in the Chthulucene is a book about multispecies entanglement on a damaged planet. Its central counsel is to refuse two temptations equally: faith in the technological fix, and the bitter cynicism that declares the game already over. Between them lies the work of sympoiesis, making-with, the composing of odd kin across species, technologies and histories. Haraway is also, famously, the author of “A Cyborg Manifesto” (1985), which refused the romance of an organic wholeness lost to machines and insisted that we are already chimeras of flesh and technology, with all the political responsibility that entails.

Measured against its source, the essay keeps faith with half the counsel and breaks it with the other. It rightly refuses the technological fix — Schmidt’s fantasy of therapy on tap. But it leans, through Putnam, Han, a steep and unquantified rise in loneliness, the shuttered humanities, and Arendt on loneliness as the soil of totalitarianism, toward the very jeremiad Haraway warns against: the narrative of decline, of a humanity being lost, of shared space being destroyed. The movement from a parcel-delivery survey to the conditions of totalitarianism in a few paragraphs is the rhetoric of the elegy, not of the kin-maker. Haraway asks us to stay in the mess and make something with what is there, including machines. The essay asks us to mourn what the machines are taking.

The irony deepens on the question of structure. Haraway’s tentacular thinking is profoundly relational but nowhere anti-structural; her string figures — cat’s cradle — are her central image of collaborative thought, and a string figure is a pattern made by rule-governed passing of form from hand to hand. It is neither baggy nor boxed. It is a game with constraints that two players hold together, each able to change the figure only by honouring what the other made. Had the essay taken its title seriously, it would have found in Haraway not an ally against structure but a model of structure made-with rather than imposed-upon — which is the very thing the audit of jackets (the Ruling, above) requires.

To the first objection the essay does counsel presence to uncertainty, and that is Haraway’s counsel in part. But staying with the trouble is not staying away from the machine, nor staying among one’s own. It is staying with the other — the alien, the strange kin, the method one dislikes, the drone in the next chair — and making something with them that neither could make alone.

The Ruling on MowlesWhat stands, what falls, and what the essay teaches against itself

In brief 2 min read
  • Stands: the critique of frictionless machine intimacy; knowledge is not wisdom; the portrait of over-facilitation; the second-order nature of problems.
  • Falls: the implicit metaphysics that treats structure as the enemy of the human encounter.
  • Lesson: each of his strongest examples depends on form.

What stands

Much stands, and stands high. The critique of frictionless machine intimacy is serious, humane and timely. The distinction between knowledge and phronetic judgement is the right weapon against the Schmidt fantasy, and it is wielded with care. The portrait of over-facilitated process — the applause cued, the outputs read aloud, the dots on the wall, the illusion of participation — is accurate and widely recognised, and Snowden concurs. The insistence that second-order problems cannot be solved by first-order technique is a genuine insight. And the essay’s honesty — about inclusion and exclusion in the beloved conference, about the author’s own drone-like compliance — is rarer than it should be and deserves respect.

What falls

What falls is the essay’s implicit metaphysics of structure. The chain from chatbot to workshop to Jackson convicts by family likeness (Question XI). The conference it loves is a liturgy, not a void, and inherits a rigorously framed lineage it never declares (Question XII). Its finest example, therapy, is the most framed encounter in human practice and argues for reliable form as the condition of spontaneity (Question XIII). Its authorities — Rosa, Han, Jaeggi — read whole, stand closer to a theory of enabling conditions than to a rejection of design (Questions XIV and XVI). Jackson’s emancipatory question is redirected rather than answered (Question XV). Going nowhere is presented as a method when it is also a privilege (Question XVI). The trouble it counsels us to stay with is selectively honoured — in one’s own room and not in another’s (Question XVII). And its title is borrowed from a thinker who would refuse both its elegy and its suspicion of the machine (Question XVIII).

What the essay teaches against itself

Every one of Mowles’s strongest moments proves that the human encounter he defends depends on form: the therapeutic frame, the conference liturgy, the group-analytic discipline of noticing, Rosa’s axes of resonance, Haraway’s string figures. His enemy is not structure but alien structure — structure he did not grow up inside, structure that is visible because it is not yet skin. That is the predicate in perfect form. Snowden calls Mowles’s jacket baggy. Mowles calls the facilitator’s jacket a cage. Each is right about the other’s garment, and each is silent about his own.

Part FiveThe three jackets: a closing triangulation

In brief 3 min read
  • Three jackets: design (anthro-complexity), tradition (complex responsive processes), and method-choice (critical systems thinking).
  • None of them can be taken off. The aim is to make each one legible, contestable and changeable by those who wear it.
  • Proposed opening rite for Hull: each tradition first describes itself as the other two see it.

Three traditions would stand in the room that Snowden proposes to convene in Hull, each wearing a jacket and each seeing the others’ jackets more clearly than its own; it is worth naming them with care.

The jacket of design belongs to anthro-complexity. Its buckles are explicit: roles, sequences, group sizes, timings, principles. Its virtue is visibility — anyone can read the method and contest it. Its vice is authorship hidden behind self-effacement: the convener stays out of the content, and the designer remains in every turn of it. It perceives the jacket of tradition as bagginess, because tradition’s buckles are not written down.

The jacket of tradition belongs to complex responsive processes. Its buckles are internal: a canon, a vocabulary, a group-analytic discipline of noticing, a liturgy of supper, keynote and open talk. Its virtue is depth — constraint practised until it becomes improvisational freedom, as in the jazz Snowden himself invokes. Its vice is invisibility to itself, and therefore a high and unspoken price of entry. It perceives the jacket of design as a cage, because design’s buckles are foreign.

The jacket of method-choice belongs to critical systems thinking. Its buckles are meta-methodological: a commitment to choosing among methods according to context and to emancipatory purpose, and to protecting those with least voice. Its virtue is pluralism and political seriousness. Its vice — the vice this imprint has elsewhere called the connoisseurship of partial truths — is to stand above the methods as their curator, as though the curator’s own position were not itself a jacket. It perceives both of the others as partial, because it is tailored to see partiality and not its own.

None of these jackets can be removed. The fantasy of a room without one — the purely open conversation, the perfectly neutral design, the view from above all methods — is the single shared illusion of all three. The adult question is not which jacket is true. It is how jackets can be made legible, contestable and changeable by those who wear them, while they are worn. That is the audit of jackets proposed in the Ruling, extended now to all three houses: let each declare its governing, enabling and constitutive constraints; let each name its author; let each give the newcomer the means to see the walls and to move them.

Were the meeting in Hull to happen, this disputation would propose one simple rite to open it. Before any method begins, each tradition describes not the other two, but itself as the other two see it. Anthro-complexity describes its own method as a cage. Complex responsive processes describes its own conference as a club. Critical systems thinking describes its own pluralism as a throne. Only then — when each has worn the others’ eyes — would the conversation be ready to begin, in whatever form the three could make together. That is staying with the trouble. That is neither baggy nor boxed. That is the string figure passed from hand to hand.

Interlude at the Threshold

Each of us wears a jacket, and the reason we cannot see it is the reason we cannot see our own backs: we are inside it. Those across the room see it clearly, because they are not; and we, in turn, see theirs. The designer names his walls scaffolding, the traditionalist names his walls freedom, the pluralist names his walls perspective, and each name is true from within and a disguise from without.

It is therefore idle to ask a gathering to have no walls; such a request can only be answered with a pretence. The serious request is different: show me where your walls are, tell me who raised them, and let me know whether I may move them. Bagginess is what a jacket calls another’s skin; confinement is what skin calls another’s jacket.

The master, when at last one meets him, is not the one who has abolished form but the one in whom form has become a body — and who retains the humility to remember that it was once a garment he was given to wear. 道 the way is worn by walking it; 理 the grain is read before the blade is lifted; 勢 the moment is met when it arrives.

Part SixThe ontology of the jacket: tacit form, the invisible tool, and the thought collective

In brief 16 min read

  • Heidegger: form becomes visible only in breakdown, so people a structure fails will always describe it most vividly.
  • Merleau-Ponty: habit incorporates form over years, so comparing a tradition with a one-off method compares a body with a garment.
  • Fleck: each thought collective sees its own style as reality and other styles as arbitrary.
  • Gadamer and MacIntyre: tradition is an argument that stays alive only while it is put at risk.
  • Kant and Hegel: freedom exists where form is both owned and articulable.

TakeawayThe asymmetry is not a vice of the two authors. It is built into how every embodied, socialised knower relates to the forms that make knowing possible.

The first five parts of this disputation have pressed a single predicate against two essays and found that it holds against both: each author perceives the other’s constraints with precision and his own with something close to blindness. It remains to ask why this should be so. An observation repeated often enough begins to demand an explanation, and an explanation that rests only on the vanity or partisanship of individual authors would be both uncharitable and shallow. Snowden and Mowles are serious and reflective practitioners; neither is a fool, and neither lacks self-awareness. If both nonetheless exhibit the same asymmetry of perception, the cause is unlikely to lie in their characters. It is more likely to lie in the structure of perception itself — in the way any embodied, socialised knower stands in relation to the forms that make his knowing possible. This part attempts that deeper account. It draws on five bodies of thought which, though rarely brought together, converge with surprising precision on the phenomenon at issue: Michael Polanyi’s account of tacit knowledge, Martin Heidegger’s analysis of equipment, Maurice Merleau-Ponty’s phenomenology of habit, Ludwik Fleck’s sociology of thought collectives, and the hermeneutic and virtue-theoretic accounts of tradition developed by Hans-Georg Gadamer and Alasdair MacIntyre. It closes with the older philosophical question that lies beneath all of them — the relation between law and freedom — as Kant and Hegel framed it.

§ 1. That we know more than we can tell

Polanyi began his account of tacit knowing — first given as the Terry Lectures at Yale in 1962 and published in 1966 as The Tacit Dimension — from a fact he stated in words that have since become proverbial: “we can know more than we can tell.” His examples were deliberately humble. We recognise a face among thousands without being able to say how; we ride a bicycle by continuous corrections we could not specify; the skilled diagnostician perceives a pattern in symptoms before she can name the features that compose it. Polanyi’s analysis distinguished two kinds of awareness. There is focal awareness, directed at the object of attention, and subsidiary awareness, by which we attend from particulars to the whole they jointly signify. When I drive a nail, I am focally aware of the nail and subsidiarily aware of the feeling of the hammer in my palm; should I shift my attention to my palm, the hammering falters. Subsidiary particulars function precisely by not being attended to. To make them focal is to destroy their function.

The bearing of this on our predicate is immediate. The constraints of a mature practice are, overwhelmingly, held in subsidiary awareness. The members of Mowles’s research community attend from their shared vocabulary, their tacit norms of turn-taking and interpretation, their sense of what counts as a good contribution, to the conversation those norms make possible. They are not focally aware of the norms — and could not be, while conversing, without the conversation faltering, exactly as the hammering falters when the carpenter attends to his palm. Hence the experience of freedom: what is held subsidiarily is not experienced as constraint at all, but as the medium through which one acts. The newcomer, by contrast, has not yet interiorised the norms, and so they press upon him focally, as obstacles: he notices that he has spoken at the wrong moment, or that his remark has been received coolly, without knowing why. The same norms are freedom to the member and constraint to the newcomer. That is not a difference in the norms. It is a difference in the mode of awareness in which they are held.

The designer’s constraints present the mirror image. Roles, sequences and timings announced at the beginning of a triopticon are, by their nature, focal: they are written, explained, displayed. Participants must attend to them before they can attend from them, and in a single event they rarely have time to make the transition. The design is therefore experienced as constraint — as a set of objects in the room — rather than as a medium. To the designer, who has run the method many times, the same roles have long since passed into subsidiary awareness; he attends from them to the dynamics they make visible, and experiences them as liberating. The participant who meets them for the first time experiences them as a box. Polanyi’s distinction thus explains both halves of the twin predicate at once. Each party experiences his own constraints subsidiarily, as freedom, and the other’s focally, as either formlessness (because the other’s constraints are not displayed) or confinement (because they are displayed and not yet interiorised).

One consequence deserves emphasis, because it bears on the proposal for a symmetrical audit with which this disputation will conclude. If subsidiary particulars function only by not being attended to, then the demand that a tradition declare its tacit constraints is not innocent. To make the tacit focal is to disturb it. A community asked to name its doxa may find, for a time, that it can no longer converse as fluently as before. Polanyi’s own response to this difficulty was not to abandon reflection but to recognise that tacit knowing can be re-interiorised at a higher level after it has been made explicit: the pianist who analyses her fingering must then forget the analysis in order to play again, but she plays better for having made it. The audit of jackets, then, must be understood not as a permanent exposure of every constraint, but as a periodic movement from subsidiary to focal and back again — an oscillation that a mature practice can survive and from which it can learn.

§ 2. The tool that withdraws

Heidegger’s analysis of equipment in Being and Time (1927, §§15–16) approaches the same phenomenon from the side of the object rather than the knower. Things encountered in everyday dealings are, in his vocabulary, ready-to-hand (zuhanden). The hammer in use is not an object of contemplation; it withdraws into the work, becoming transparent, so that what is present to the worker is the task rather than the tool. The hammer becomes conspicuous only when it fails. Heidegger names three modes of this failure: conspicuousness, when the tool is damaged and cannot be used; obtrusiveness, when a needed tool is missing; and obstinacy, when something stands in the way of the work. In each case the equipment, previously transparent, lights up as an object, and with it the whole referential network of the workshop briefly becomes visible.

A gathering’s form is equipment in exactly this sense. In smooth functioning it withdraws; participants are absorbed in the conversation, not in the structure that holds it. The form becomes visible only in breakdown. Mowles’s account of the facilitated workshop is, read through Heidegger, a phenomenology of obstinacy: the structure stood in the way of what he took the work to be, and so it lit up, became an object, became oppressive. His account of his own conference is a phenomenology of readiness-to-hand: its structure withdrew into the conversation it supported, and was therefore available to him only as freedom. The newcomer’s experience of exclusion, which Mowles honestly reports, is the moment at which the conference’s equipment becomes conspicuous for someone else — the moment its tacit structure fails to work for a person not yet fitted to it.

Heidegger’s account adds something Polanyi’s does not: an explanation of why the visibility of form tends to coincide with resentment of it. Form becomes visible precisely when it is not working for the one who perceives it. Hence structure is very often first encountered as an obstacle, and its enabling character is noticed, if at all, only retrospectively. This produces a systematic bias in testimony. Those for whom a structure works will report the experience of the task; those for whom it fails will report the experience of the structure. Accounts of facilitated workshops written by people who found them obstinate will therefore always be more vivid, more detailed and more structural than accounts written by people for whom the same workshops worked. The same is true, symmetrically, of open gatherings: the newcomer who felt excluded will describe the hidden rules, while the delighted regular will describe only the conversation. Any comparison of the two kinds of gathering that relies on participants’ testimony must correct for this bias; neither essay does so, and Mowles’s, which rests entirely on his own testimony of one event of each kind, is especially exposed to it.

§ 3. Habit as the incorporation of form

Merleau-Ponty’s Phenomenology of Perception (1945) carries the analysis into the body. His celebrated examples — the woman who keeps a safe distance between the feather in her hat and the doorframe without calculating it, the blind man for whom the cane has ceased to be an object and become an extension of touch, the organist who, in an hour of rehearsal on an unfamiliar instrument, comes to inhabit its stops and pedals as if they were his own limbs — all illustrate what he called the incorporation of instruments into the body schema. Habit, for Merleau-Ponty, is neither mechanical reflex nor intellectual rule; it is the body’s acquisition of a new power, a reorganisation of its motor significance such that the world now solicits it differently. The feather, the cane and the organ are no longer things the body uses; they are part of how the body is in the world.

Two consequences follow for our question. The first is that interiorised form is not merely invisible; it is generative. The organist who has incorporated the instrument does not merely play without noticing the stops; he can now improvise, because the instrument has become a range of possible movement rather than a set of obstacles. This is the precise phenomenological content of the claim made in Question IV, that the jazz musician’s, the cook’s and the martial artist’s freedom rests on incorporated constraint. It is also the content of Goethe’s tercet. Limitation, once incorporated, ceases to be limitation and becomes capacity.

The second consequence is less comfortable for either party. Incorporation takes time, and its pace is governed by the body, not by the design. Merleau-Ponty’s organist needed an hour; a group-analytic practitioner needs years; a facilitated method encountered once needs at least as long as the method takes to run, and probably much longer. This is the deepest reason why Snowden’s triopticon cannot simply be offered as an improvement upon a tradition’s open conversation. The tradition’s form has been incorporated; the method’s form has not. Comparing the two at a single event compares a body with a garment. Conversely, it is the deepest reason why Mowles’s dismissal of the facilitated workshop is premature. A method experienced once has not yet had the chance to become a body, and what is experienced as a box may become, with practice, a range of motion. Neither essay asks what either form would feel like after it had been inhabited for a decade by people who had not grown up inside it. That is the question that would actually decide between them.

§ 4. Thought collectives and the arbitrariness of others

If Polanyi, Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty explain the asymmetry at the level of individual perception, Ludwik Fleck explains it at the level of communities. Fleck’s Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact (1935), a study of how the Wassermann reaction came to be accepted as a test for syphilis, introduced two concepts that anticipated much of later sociology of knowledge: the thought collective (Denkkollektiv), a community of persons mutually exchanging ideas and maintaining intellectual interaction; and the thought style (Denkstil), the readiness for directed perception which such a collective cultivates in its members. A thought style determines not only what its bearers think but what they see: what counts as a fact, a problem, an anomaly, a relevant observation.

Fleck’s most penetrating observation concerns how collectives perceive one another. Within a thought collective, the style is experienced not as a style but as reality: its assumptions appear as obvious, its methods as natural, its perceptions as simply what is there. The thought styles of other collectives, by contrast, appear arbitrary, mystical or confused. Fleck noted that communication between collectives is therefore systematically distorted; ideas passing from one collective to another are transformed, often beyond recognition, because the receiving collective assimilates them to its own style. He distinguished, too, between the esoteric circle of specialists at the heart of a collective and the wider exoteric circle of the educated public, and observed that a style is most rigid, and least visible to itself, at the esoteric core.

It would be hard to find a more exact description of the present exchange. Anthro-complexity, complex responsive processes and critical systems thinking are thought collectives in Fleck’s sense, each with a distinctive style: a readiness to perceive groups as fields of constraint and entrainment; a readiness to perceive them as figurations of power and identity in continuous negotiation; a readiness to perceive them as sites for the selection of methods in the service of emancipation. Each perceives its own style as simply attending to what is there, and each perceives the others’ as arbitrary. Snowden sees Mowles’s conference as baggy because his style perceives constraint only where it has been designed; Mowles sees the workshop as a cage because his style perceives design as the suppression of emergent relating; Jackson, on both men’s accounts, sees complex responsive processes as insufficiently protective of the weak because his style perceives deliberation through the lens of emancipatory method. Fleck would add that the transformations Jackson’s position has undergone in passing through Mowles and then Snowden — the ventriloquism noted in Question XV — are exactly what one should expect when an idea travels between collectives.

Fleck offers one further resource. He held that the most productive moments in the history of thought occur where collectives overlap — where an individual belongs to several and must hold their styles in tension. Such persons are, in his account, the carriers of stylistic change. This suggests a more modest and more fruitful aim for the proposed meeting in Hull than the adjudication Snowden envisages. Its value would lie not in deciding which style is correct, a question Fleck would regard as malformed, but in creating conditions under which some participants might come to inhabit more than one style at once, and so perceive their own as a style for the first time.

§ 5. Tradition as argument; prejudice as condition

The charge that Mowles’s conference is a club rests on an implicit theory of tradition: that a tradition is a body of shared assumptions, and that people who share assumptions are thereby less able to think well than people who do not. Two of the most important philosophical accounts of tradition in the twentieth century reject this theory, and their rejection bears directly on the dispute.

Gadamer’s Truth and Method (1960) rehabilitated the concept of prejudice (Vorurteil), literally a pre-judgement, against the Enlightenment’s blanket suspicion of it. Understanding, Gadamer argued, is never presuppositionless; it proceeds from the fore-structures that tradition supplies, and the attempt to purge oneself of all prejudice is itself a prejudice — the Enlightenment’s prejudice against prejudice. What distinguishes productive from distorting prejudice is not whether one has it but whether it can be put at risk in the encounter with what is other. Gadamer’s model of understanding is the fusion of horizons: the interpreter’s horizon, shaped by tradition, meets the horizon of the text or the other person, and both are transformed. The interpreter does not leave her horizon behind; she brings it into play. On this account, a community that shares a tradition is not thereby disabled from thought; it is enabled for it, provided that its tradition is held in a way that remains open to being questioned.

MacIntyre’s After Virtue (1981) and Whose Justice? Which Rationality? (1988) go further. A living tradition, MacIntyre wrote, is “an historically extended, socially embodied argument” — an argument, precisely in part, about the goods which constitute that tradition. Traditions are not bodies of agreement but continuities of conflict; when a tradition ceases to argue about its own goods, it is dying. MacIntyre also offered an account of how traditions are rationally vindicated against one another: a tradition shows its superiority when it can explain, in its own terms, why a rival tradition encountered the difficulties it did, and can do so in a way that the rival’s own adherents can come to recognise. He called the crisis that provokes such encounters an epistemological crisis, and held that traditions grow by passing through them.

Applied to the present case, these accounts rebut the club charge and indict the club at once. They rebut it because a long-standing community of practice is, on MacIntyre’s account, likely to be a site of sustained argument rather than mere agreement — as the out-group homogeneity effect discussed in Part Three would lead one to expect, its members will experience it as far more internally contested than outsiders suppose. They indict it, however, because both Gadamer and MacIntyre make the vitality of tradition depend upon its willingness to be put at risk. A tradition that converses only with itself, however vigorously, has not yet met its epistemological crisis. Mowles’s essay, read in this light, displays a tradition confident in its own goods and disinclined to imagine that a rival — whether Jackson’s pluralism or Snowden’s design — might explain its difficulties better than it can explain them itself. The proper response to the club charge is therefore not denial but invitation: to seek out precisely those encounters in which the tradition’s horizon might be fused with another, at the risk of its own transformation. It is, in fact, the very encounter Snowden proposes; the difficulty, as Question VII argued, is only that he proposes to stage it within his own horizon.

§ 6. The law that gives freedom

Beneath all these accounts lies a question as old as moral philosophy, and Goethe’s epigraph gestures at it without pursuing it: in what sense can law give freedom? Two answers from the German tradition illuminate the present dispute with particular clarity.

Kant’s answer, in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785), is autonomy. Freedom is not the absence of law but the giving of law to oneself; the will that obeys only its own rational legislation is free, while the will that is moved by external causes, even pleasant ones, is heteronomous. On this account, the decisive question about any constraint is not how tight it is but whose law it is. A constraint one has given oneself — or could give oneself, as a rational agent reflecting on what one would will as a universal law — is the expression of freedom; a constraint merely imposed is its negation. The bearing on the present case is direct. The open conference’s constraints are, for its members, largely autonomous in Kant’s sense: they have been taken up and endorsed over years. The triopticon’s constraints are, for a first-time participant, heteronomous: they are given by a designer. But the newcomer to the conference meets its constraints heteronomously too, and the seasoned practitioner of the triopticon may have made its form his own. Autonomy is not a property of forms; it is a relation between a form and those who live under it. Kant thus supplies the criterion that the audit of jackets requires: not “how much structure?” nor even “what kind?” but “could those who live under this form recognise it as their own law?”

Hegel’s answer, in the Philosophy of Right (1820), corrects Kant in a way that bears equally on the dispute. Hegel thought Kantian autonomy too abstract, too individual; the self that gives law to itself alone is empty, for it has no content but the form of universality. Real freedom, Hegel argued, is realised only in ethical life (Sittlichkeit): in institutions — family, civil society, state — whose customs and practices individuals recognise as the expression of their own will, and in which they find themselves at home. Freedom is being with oneself in another (bei sich selbst sein in einem Anderen). On this account, the open conference at its best is a small instance of Sittlichkeit: a set of customs in which its members are at home, and through which they are free. But Hegel also insisted that ethical life must be rational — that its customs must be capable of being articulated, criticised and justified, or else they collapse into mere habit, and freedom into conformity. The danger of a tradition that does not declare its constraints is precisely that its Sittlichkeit decays into what Hegel called mere positivity: custom without reason, at home only for those who were born into it.

Kant and Hegel together therefore supply the philosophical foundation for the position this disputation has been approaching. Against Snowden, they hold that freedom is not secured by better design, because a design not recognised as one’s own law is heteronomous however enabling it may be. Against Mowles, they hold that freedom is not secured by custom alone, because custom not articulated and justified decays into positivity and excludes those who did not grow up within it. Freedom is secured where form is both owned and articulable: where those who live under it can recognise it as their own and can also give an account of it to those who do not yet share it. That double condition is the subject of Part Nine.

Third Interlude · On the Visibility of Forms

The forms that hold us become visible in three circumstances only: when they fail us, when they are new to us, and when someone outside them describes them to us. In the first we resent them, in the second we resist them, and in the third we are inclined to deny that they exist. It is a rare and difficult discipline to perceive one’s own form while it is working, as an old form, in one’s own words. Yet without that discipline every dispute about structure becomes what this one has been: a quarrel between people who can each see only the other’s garment.

Part SevenThe carpenter’s square: the classical Chinese debate on ritual, nature and form

TakeawayThe classical tradition located the answer in the formation of those who gather, not in the design of events.

It would be parochial to suppose that the quarrel between the baggy and the boxed was invented in the business schools of Hertfordshire and Hull, or in the consultancies that grew up around them. The most sustained and subtle argument about the relation between imposed form and spontaneous conduct in any civilisation was conducted in China between the sixth and third centuries before the common era, among the thinkers later grouped as Confucian, Mohist, Daoist and Legalist. That argument concerned ritual (禮 lǐ), human nature (性 xìng), artifice (偽 wěi), spontaneity (自然 zìrán) and the pattern inherent in things (理 lǐ). It was conducted with a philosophical seriousness and a literary brilliance that the present exchange, for all its merits, does not approach, and it reached conclusions that bear upon our question with uncanny directness. This part traces five moments in that argument. The translations are the author’s own.

§ 1. At seventy, the square became desire

The most famous autobiographical passage in the Analects (2.4) traces a life as a sequence of stages:

吾十有五而志于學，三十而立，四十而不惑，五十而知天命，六十而耳順，七十而從心所欲，不踰矩。At fifteen I set my heart on learning; at thirty I took my stand; at forty I was free of doubts; at fifty I knew the decree of Heaven; at sixty my ear was attuned; at seventy I could follow what my heart desired without overstepping the square.

The last word is the crux. 矩 jǔ is the carpenter’s square, the tool by which right angles are drawn and true lines tested; by extension, the norm or measure. What Confucius describes at seventy is not liberation from the square but its complete incorporation: desire and measure have become one. He can follow his heart because his heart has been shaped, over fifty-five years, into the form of the square. It is the most compressed statement in any literature of the condition this disputation has called the jacket become body.

Three features of the passage bear on the present dispute. First, the incorporation of the square takes a lifetime. It is not achieved at a weekend conference or in a one-day workshop; its stages are measured in decades. Any comparison between forms of gathering that ignores the time of formation, as Part Six argued, compares the incomparable. Second, the passage locates freedom at the end of a long submission to form, not at its beginning. The young Confucius set his heart on learning — on the study of ritual, music and the classics — and took his stand within them; only at the end does constraint become indistinguishable from spontaneity. This is a direct rebuke to any romanticism of the uncontrived. What looks like unconstrained conduct in the master is the fruit of the most thorough discipline. Third, and against any easy appropriation by the designers: the square Confucius incorporates is not a method devised for an occasion but a tradition received and made his own. The Analects are explicit that he transmitted rather than invented (7.1: 述而不作, “I transmit; I do not create”). The freedom of the seventy-year-old is the freedom of a tradition inhabited, not of a technique applied.

§ 2. Rustic and clerk: the ancient names of baggy and boxed

If the predicate of this disputation has an ancient formulation, it is Analects 6.18:

質勝文則野，文勝質則史。文質彬彬，然後君子。When substance prevails over refinement, one is rustic; when refinement prevails over substance, one is a clerk. Only when refinement and substance are in due proportion is one a person of quality.

The two failures Confucius names are, almost exactly, the two that Snowden’s title names. 野 yě — literally “the wilds”, the uncultivated land beyond the city walls — names the person in whom natural substance has received no form: sincere perhaps, but unpolished, unpredictable, governed by impulse. That is the baggy. 史 shǐ — the scribe, the archivist, the clerk of ceremonies — names the person in whom form has outrun substance: correct in every observance, and hollow. That is the boxed. And the remedy Confucius proposes is not a third thing placed between them but a proportion within a person: 彬彬 bīnbīn, an evenly blended quality in which form and substance are fused.

The passage illuminates the dispute in two ways. It confirms, first, that the opposition is perennial and that its terms are relational rather than absolute. Rustic and clerk are judged from the standpoint of a cultivated person, and what counts as rustic depends upon the refinement against which it is measured; a village elder may be rustic to a court official and the court official a clerk to a sage. This is our predicate in Confucian dress. It shows, second, that the classical answer to the opposition was not to design a method that would sit between rusticity and clerkliness, but to form persons — and communities of persons — in whom the two were reconciled. The place where baggy and boxed are overcome is not the event; it is the character.

Two further passages sharpen this. Asked about the root of ritual (3.4), Confucius replies that in ceremony it is better to be sparing than extravagant, and in mourning it is better to grieve deeply than to have every observance in perfect order. Form serves substance, and where they conflict, substance takes precedence. And at 3.3 he asks what a person without humaneness (仁 rén) could possibly have to do with ritual or with music. Ritual without the inner quality it is meant to express is not merely useless but a kind of fraud. Both passages caution the designers: a form that does not express and cultivate a substance its participants already possess, or are coming to possess, is clerkly however ingenious. Both passages caution the traditionalists too, for they presuppose that ritual is indispensable; the question is never whether to have it, but whether it is animated.

§ 3. Sprouts and the press-frame: Mencius against Xunzi

The Confucian tradition divided, within two centuries of its founder, over the question that lies beneath the present dispute: is goodness in human beings something that grows of itself when not obstructed, or something that must be imposed upon a recalcitrant nature by deliberate artifice?

Mencius (fourth century BCE) held the first view. Human beings, he argued (2A6), possess four sprouts (四端 sì duān) — of compassion, of shame, of deference and of discernment — as surely as they possess four limbs. Anyone who suddenly saw a child about to fall into a well would feel alarm and concern, not from calculation but from the sprout of compassion. Moral cultivation is the nurturing of these sprouts until they become the full virtues of humaneness, rightness, propriety and wisdom. The good is native; it needs tending, not manufacturing.

Xunzi (third century BCE) held the second. The chapter of his writings titled “Human Nature Is Bad” (性惡, ch. 23) opens with a thesis designed to scandalise Mencius’s followers: human nature is bad, and its goodness is the product of artifice (偽 wěi). Left to themselves, human beings are born with a love of profit, envy and the desires of the senses; follow them, and strife and disorder ensue. Goodness is the work of the sage-kings, who devised ritual and rightness to order these desires. Xunzi’s governing image is drawn from carpentry:

故枸木必將待檃栝烝矯然後直，鈍金必將待礱厲然後利。Thus warped wood must await the press-frame and steaming before it becomes straight; blunt metal must await the whetstone before it becomes sharp.

The press-frame (檃栝 yǐnkuò) is a device for straightening timber by sustained pressure. Ritual, for Xunzi, is the press-frame of human nature: an external structure, deliberately designed, which, applied over time, produces a straightness that nature alone would never achieve. And crucially, Xunzi argued in his chapter on ritual (禮論, ch. 19) that the rites arose because the ancient kings, hating disorder, established them in order to apportion and to nurture desires — to give each person a measure. Ritual is designed; it is the product of sagely intelligence responding to a problem.

The correspondence with the present dispute is close and instructive, though not exact. Mowles stands nearer to Mencius: his confidence that something valuable emerges when conversation is not obstructed, that the novel and the spontaneous will sprout if space is left for them, is a Mencian confidence in the native goods of relating. Snowden stands nearer to Xunzi: his conviction that unstructured groups fall prey to entrainment and the domination of recognised power, and that carefully designed constraints are needed to produce fair and generative deliberation, is a Xunzian diagnosis met with a Xunzian remedy. The triopticon is a press-frame. Its roles and sequences are wěi — artifice devised by a practitioner-sage in response to disorder.

But the classical debate also shows what both modern parties omit. Xunzi’s press-frame works only by sustained application: the warped wood must be held and steamed over time. A press-frame applied for an afternoon straightens nothing. And Xunzi was emphatic that the purpose of ritual is to transform the person until the artifice becomes second nature — until, as he put it elsewhere, the gentleman’s learning enters through the ear, is fixed in the heart, spreads through the four limbs and appears in his conduct. Snowden’s method is Xunzian in its diagnosis but not in its temporality: it offers the press-frame without the years of steaming. Mencius, for his part, never held that sprouts flourish untended. His sprouts require deliberate cultivation, a nurture conducted within the ritual forms that he no less than Xunzi took for granted. Mowles’s Mencianism is selective: it trusts the sprouts but forgets the tending.

§ 4. The farmer of Song: neither neglect nor forcing

Mencius supplies, in a single parable, the classical rebuke to both parties. In the course of a long discussion of how the flood-like qì is to be nurtured (2A2), he tells of a man of Song who, worried that his sprouts were not growing, went out and pulled at them to help them along. He came home exhausted and told his family that he had been helping the sprouts grow. His son ran out to look, and found the sprouts withered. Mencius draws the moral with precision. There are few in the world who do not help their sprouts to grow in this way. Those who think nurture is of no use, and so abandon the field, are those who do not weed. Those who try to help growth along are those who pull at sprouts. Not only does this not help — it does harm.

Two errors are named here, and they correspond, again with precision, to the twin predicates. The one who abandons the field and does not weed is the baggy error: the conviction that growth will occur of itself if only one stays out of the way. The one who pulls at sprouts is the boxed error: the conviction that growth can be produced by direct intervention on the thing that grows. Neither, for Mencius, is cultivation. Cultivation is weeding — the removal of obstacles, the tending of conditions, the patient attention that neither abandons nor forces.

The bearing on the present dispute could hardly be stronger. Snowden’s best insight — that enabling constraints shape conditions without determining outcomes — is a theory of weeding. His method’s risk, as Questions IV and V argued, is that its elaborate choreography of roles and phases becomes a pulling at sprouts: an attempt to produce emergence by specifying in advance the steps through which it must pass. Mowles’s best insight — that the novel arises in gaps and frustrations that cannot be planned — is a recognition that sprouts grow of themselves. His essay’s risk, as Questions XV and XVI argued, is that it abandons the field: that in refusing to pull, it also refuses to weed, leaving the weak plants to be shaded out by the strong. The farmer of Song stands as a warning to the designer, and the farmer who will not weed as a warning to the traditionalist. The figure Mencius commends — the patient cultivator who attends to conditions without forcing outcomes — is neither of them. It is, perhaps, what both are trying to be.

§ 5. Cook Ding and the hollows of the ox

The Daoist tradition entered this debate as a critic of Confucian ritual as such, and its critique deserves to be heard, for it is the strongest version of Mowles’s case. The Zhuangzi, in the chapter titled “Webbed Toes” (駢拇, ch. 8), attacks the imposition of measure upon nature with an image as memorable as Xunzi’s press-frame:

鳧脛雖短，續之則憂；鶴脛雖長，斷之則悲。Though the duck’s legs are short, to lengthen them would grieve it; though the crane’s legs are long, to cut them would bring it sorrow.

The same chapter declares that whatever must await the hook, the line, the compass and the square to be made correct has had its nature pared away. Here the carpenter’s square of Analects 2.4 returns as an instrument of violence. Every imposed measure, on this view, deforms the thing measured; the rites and the virtues of the Confucians are so many attempts to lengthen ducks and shorten cranes. The facilitated workshop Mowles describes is precisely such a procedure: participants of every temperament and gift processed through a uniform sequence, their contributions trimmed to fit the flip chart and the dot. The Zhuangzian critique of the boxed is unanswerable on its own ground.

Yet the Zhuangzi does not stop at the critique of imposed form, and here it parts company with any romanticism of the formless. In the chapter on nourishing life (養生主, ch. 3) it offers the portrait of Cook Ding, who carves an ox for Lord Wenhui with such grace that every movement of his hand and shoulder, every thrust of his knee, is in rhythm, as if he were performing an ancient dance. The lord marvels at his skill; Ding replies that what he cares for is the Way, which goes beyond skill. When he began, he saw nothing but the whole ox; after three years he no longer saw the whole ox; now he meets it with his spirit rather than looking at it with his eyes. He relies on the heavenly pattern (依乎天理), striking into the great hollows and guiding the blade through the great openings, following what is inherently so. A good cook changes his knife once a year, because he cuts; an ordinary cook changes it once a month, because he hacks. Ding has had his knife for nineteen years, and its edge is as fresh as if it had just come from the whetstone, because there are spaces between the joints and the blade has no thickness; inserting what has no thickness into what has space, there is ample room for the blade to play.

This is not formlessness. Cook Ding’s freedom is the freedom of one who has learned, over nineteen years, to perceive the lǐ — the inherent pattern — of the ox, and to move in accordance with it. His form is not imposed upon the ox, as the Confucian rites are imposed upon nature in the critique of “Webbed Toes”; nor is it absent, as in the hacking of the ordinary cook. It is a form discovered in the material and incorporated into the body, until carving becomes dance. This is the third possibility that neither of the modern essays articulates: neither design imposed upon a group, nor openness left to whatever forces happen to prevail, but a disciplined perception of the pattern already latent in a particular group at a particular moment, and a responsiveness to it. It is what Part One’s coda called reading the grain before the blade is lifted.

And the parable contains one more detail, which might have been written for Mowles’s title. Ding continues: even so, whenever he comes to a knot — a place where things are tangled — he sees its difficulty, becomes wary and alert, his gaze settles, his movements slow, and he moves the blade with the greatest delicacy, until with a flop the parts separate like a clod of earth crumbling to the ground. This is staying with the trouble in its most exact sense: not the abandonment of skill in the face of difficulty, nor its mechanical application, but the master’s heightened attention at precisely the point where the pattern resists. The knot is where the master slows down. It is not where he throws away the knife.

§ 6. The usefulness of the empty room

The Daodejing supplies the image with which this part may close, for it reconciles the two halves of the predicate in a single figure. Chapter 11 reads:

三十輻共一轂，當其無，有車之用。埏埴以為器，當其無，有器之用。鑿戶牖以為室，當其無，有室之用。故有之以為利，無之以為用。Thirty spokes share one hub; it is in the emptiness at its centre that the cart has its use. Clay is shaped into a vessel; it is in the emptiness within that the vessel has its use. Doors and windows are cut to make a room; it is in the emptiness within that the room has its use. Thus what is there gives the benefit; what is not there gives the use.

Mowles is right that the use of a gathering lies in its emptiness — in the space left open for what has not yet been thought. A room crammed with procedure, like a vessel filled with clay, has no use. But the Daodejing is equally clear that emptiness is useful only because it is bounded. The hub’s emptiness is useful only because thirty spokes converge on it; the vessel’s emptiness only because clay surrounds it; the room’s emptiness only because walls enclose it and doors and windows have been cut. Remove the walls and there is no room, only the open field, whose emptiness is of no use to anyone seeking shelter. Snowden is right that the walls matter; Mowles is right that the emptiness matters; and Laozi’s point is that neither can be had without the other, because each is constituted by the other. 有 yǒu and 無 wú, presence and absence, form and openness, are not opposites to be balanced by a third term. They are the two aspects of a single usefulness.

The classical argument thus arrives, by several roads, at a conclusion the modern essays approach and do not reach. The opposition of baggy and boxed — of rustic and clerk, of untended field and pulled sprout, of hacked ox and pared nature — is perennial and relational. It is overcome not by a method located between its poles, but by a cultivated perception of the pattern in things (lǐ), by forms incorporated over years until they become desire (the square at seventy), by tending rather than forcing (the farmer who weeds), by heightened attention at the knot (Cook Ding), and by the recognition that bounded emptiness is the only useful emptiness (the room of chapter 11). These are not techniques. They are dispositions, formed in persons and communities over time. That is the deepest reproach the classical tradition has to offer both modern parties: that each, in his different way, has located the answer in the design or the absence of design of events, when the classical tradition located it in the formation of those who gather.

Part EightThe politics of the room: ideal speech, three faces of power, and the agonistic alternative

In brief 13 min read
  • Neither room meets Habermas’s ideal speech situation. Foucault’s aim is the more realistic one: games of power played with minimal domination, and kept reversible.
  • Lukes: the designer holds agenda power, and both rooms shape what their participants want (the third face of power).
  • Young: each room privileges one style of speech, expert exposition in one and reflexive interpretation in the other.
  • Evidence: like-minded groups polarise (Sunstein), while designed, balanced deliberation works (Fishkin, Ireland). Above all, who is in the room matters most.
  • Mouffe: real agonism needs a frame that can itself be contested, and real adversaries.

TakeawayFive conditions for a just gathering: composition, a declared and contestable form, plural modes of speech, agonistic conflict, reversible power.

Both essays are, at bottom, essays in political philosophy, though neither announces itself as such. Snowden’s case against the open room is a case about domination: that without designed constraint, recognised power determines who speaks and which ideas survive. Mowles’s case against the facilitated workshop is a case about alienation: that over-designed process reduces participants to drones, foreclosing their capacity to affect and be affected. Jackson’s question, which both essays relay and neither answers, is a case about justice: whether the disadvantaged are protected. These are the oldest questions of democratic theory, transposed into the small polity of the meeting room, and the democratic theory of the last half-century has examined them with a rigour that neither essay draws upon. This part brings that literature to bear. It considers, in turn, the Habermasian ideal of undistorted communication and the Foucauldian reply; Steven Lukes’s three dimensions of power; Iris Marion Young’s critique of deliberative norms; the empirical record of deliberative experiments; and Chantal Mouffe’s agonistic alternative.

§ 1. The dream of the power-free room

Jürgen Habermas’s theory of communicative action, developed in the 1970s and set out at length in The Theory of Communicative Action (1981), rests on the claim that every act of serious communication implicitly presupposes the possibility of reaching understanding through the force of the better argument alone. He called the idealised conditions under which such understanding could be reached the ideal speech situation: every competent subject may take part in discourse; everyone may question any assertion, introduce any assertion, and express attitudes, desires and needs; and no speaker may be prevented, by internal or external coercion, from exercising these rights. Habermas did not suppose that such a situation ever obtains. He held that it is a counterfactual presupposition of discourse itself — something we must assume in order to argue at all, and which therefore serves as a critical standard against which actual communication can be measured and found distorted.

Snowden’s triopticon can be read as an engineering approximation to the ideal speech situation. Its distribution of roles across hierarchy, its silencing of the first confident voice, its ritualisation of challenge, its insistence that the convener stay out of the content — each is a device for reducing the distortions that recognised power introduces into discourse. The aspiration is honourable and has a distinguished philosophical lineage. But Habermas’s own conditions expose the method’s limits. The ideal speech situation grants everyone the right to question any assertion at any point. The triopticon withholds speech from Ravens in its opening phase, confines challenge to the Coyote’s office, and places the form of the discourse itself beyond question, since it has been settled in advance. Measured against Habermas, the method reduces some distortions by introducing others. It is a better approximation than the sticky-dot workshop; it is not the thing itself, and its designer’s claim to neutrality, as Part Three argued, is precisely the kind of claim that Habermas’s critique of distorted communication was meant to expose.

Mowles’s open conference can be read, more surprisingly, as a different approximation to the same ideal. Its absence of designed procedure leaves every participant formally free to speak, question and introduce any topic; its reflexive habit of talking about how the group is talking is a practice of exposing distortion as it occurs. But Habermas’s analysis applies here too. Formal freedom to speak is not effective freedom, and the internal coercions that prevent a speaker from exercising her rights — diffidence, unfamiliarity with the group’s idiom, awareness of status — are exactly those that operate most powerfully in a room without designed protection. The conference approximates the ideal speech situation for its established members and falls far short of it for newcomers. Neither room, measured against Habermas, is undistorted. Each distorts differently.

§ 2. Games of power with a minimum of domination

Michel Foucault’s reply to Habermas, developed in scattered interviews in the last years of his life, was that the dream of communication free of power is not merely unattainable but incoherent. Power, for Foucault, is not a substance possessed by some and lacked by others, nor a distortion of an otherwise neutral medium; it is a relation, productive as well as repressive, present in every interaction. There is no outside of power from which communication could be purified. In an interview of 1984 on the ethics of care for the self, Foucault accepted that relations of power are inescapable but distinguished them sharply from states of domination, in which relations of power have become fixed and asymmetrical, leaving one party no room for manoeuvre. The task, he suggested, is not to dissolve power in a utopia of transparent communication, but to equip ourselves with the rules, techniques, ethics and practices of the self that allow games of power to be played with as little domination as possible.

This formulation is, in effect, the philosophical charter of the position this disputation has defended. It refuses Snowden’s implicit Habermasianism — the notion that good design can approximate a room in which the better argument alone prevails — because it denies that any room is free of power. It refuses, equally, the implicit romanticism of Mowles’s essay — the notion that leaving conversation open lets something more authentic emerge — because openness is itself a field of power relations, and may harden into domination for those who lack the resources to play. What it demands instead is attention to the reversibility of power relations in a given setting: whether those who are disadvantaged in one moment have the means to contest, resist and alter the terms of play in the next. That is the criterion by which both rooms should be judged. The question is not whether power is present, for it always is, but whether the game it structures remains open to being replayed on different terms.

Elias, whose sociology underlies complex responsive processes, would have recognised the formulation; his figurational account of power as a shifting balance within relations of interdependence is close kin to Foucault’s. This makes the silence of Mowles’s essay on the question of domination the more striking. The tradition he represents possesses, in Elias, precisely the resources to answer Jackson’s question in Foucauldian terms — to say that power in the open room is relational and negotiable rather than fixed — and to specify what practices keep it so. The essay does not use them.

§ 3. The three faces of power

Steven Lukes’s Power: A Radical View (1974; second edition 2005) offers a taxonomy that sharpens the analysis further. Lukes distinguished three dimensions of power. The first, associated with Robert Dahl, is visible in decision-making: power is exercised when A prevails over B in an observable conflict. The second, associated with Peter Bachrach and Morton Baratz, is exercised in agenda-setting: A may prevail by preventing certain issues from ever reaching the table, mobilising the bias of institutions to keep grievances latent. The third, which Lukes added, is the most insidious: A may exercise power over B by shaping B’s very wants, perceptions and preferences, so that B accepts an order of things against B’s real interests because B cannot imagine it otherwise.

Snowden’s critique of the open room operates in the first two dimensions. In the absence of structure, he argues, the senior and the confident prevail in the conversation (first dimension), and the habits of regular delegates determine which ideas are taken up and which fall away (second dimension). The triopticon is designed to counteract both. But it does so by concentrating second-dimensional power in the designer. The form of the event — what is discussed in what order, by whom, in what groupings, towards what kind of output — is an agenda in Bachrach and Baratz’s sense, settled before any participant arrives. Participants cannot raise the issue of whether the event should take this form at all; that issue has been kept off the table by the method itself. The designed room does not abolish the second face of power. It relocates it upstream.

It is in the third dimension, however, that both rooms are most exposed and least self-aware. The open conference shapes what its members want to say, what they perceive as a good contribution, what questions seem worth asking — through the tradition’s vocabulary, its canonical texts, its prized competence of talking about how one talks. Its members experience this shaping as their own authentic interest. The triopticon shapes what its participants perceive as a contribution too: something that can be expressed by an Eagle, made sense of by Ravens in trios, built upon by Beavers, clustered on a wall and owned by a named individual. A contribution that does not fit this shape — an inarticulate unease, a grief, a refusal — has no place to go. Each room exercises third-dimensional power over its participants by constituting what they recognise as worth wanting. Lukes’s third face is the political name of what Parts One and Six have called the jacket: the form that shapes desire so thoroughly that it is experienced as freedom.

§ 4. Whose speech counts

Iris Marion Young’s Inclusion and Democracy (2000) pressed a further objection against deliberative democracy, and it applies with equal force to both rooms. Theories of deliberation, Young argued, tend to privilege a particular style of speech — articulate, dispassionate, orderly, argumentative — which is unevenly distributed across social groups and correlated with privilege of education, class, gender and culture. Those who speak in other registers — passionately, through narrative, through rhetoric, through gesture — are heard as less reasonable, and their contributions discounted. Young proposed that inclusive deliberation must recognise further modes of communication: greeting, the public acknowledgement of others as participants; rhetoric, the emotional and figurative dimensions of speech that make it persuasive to particular audiences; and narrative, the telling of situated experience that makes visible what abstract argument cannot.

Measured against Young, the triopticon privileges expert exposition in its opening phase, sense-making in its middle phase and concept-building in its later phases. These are the registers of the educated professional. A participant whose most important contribution is a story of what it was like to be on the receiving end of the system under discussion has, in this sequence, no natural home; she is not an Eagle, and her story is not easily made into a cluster. The open conference, for its part, privileges the reflexive, psychoanalytically inflected register of group analysis: the capacity to notice and name group dynamics, to interpret, to tolerate ambiguity. This too is a learned style, unevenly distributed, and a participant who speaks bluntly, or tells stories rather than reflecting on process, or becomes angry, may find that her contribution is received as symptom rather than argument. Neither essay asks which modes of speech its preferred room makes room for. Young’s question is the one that would test both men’s claims to serve the least powerful.

§ 5. What the experiments show

The debate between the baggy and the boxed is not, in principle, beyond empirical test, and a substantial literature on deliberation exists that bears upon it — a literature which neither essay cites. Three findings are especially pertinent.

The first is Cass Sunstein’s “law of group polarization” (2002), drawn from a large body of social-psychological research: when like-minded people deliberate together, they tend to move towards a more extreme version of the position they held at the outset. The mechanisms are a limited argument pool, in which members hear mostly arguments that support their prior inclination, and social comparison, in which members shift to position themselves favourably relative to the perceived group norm. This finding supports Snowden’s worry about the club in a specific sense. A community that shares a tradition and deliberates largely within it is at risk not merely of stasis but of drift towards a purified version of its own assumptions. Mowles’s essay, which presents the conference as a site of productive frustration, does not consider this possibility.

The second is the record of deliberative polling, developed by James Fishkin from the late 1980s and summarised in When the People Speak (2009). A random sample of citizens is polled, given balanced briefing materials, brought together for a weekend of moderated small-group discussion and plenary sessions with competing experts, and then polled again. Fishkin’s findings, replicated in many countries, are that participants’ knowledge increases, their opinions often change significantly, and the polarisation that Sunstein’s law predicts does not generally occur. The difference lies in the design: balanced information, trained moderators, heterogeneous groups. This evidence supports Snowden’s central claim — that well-designed structure can produce better deliberation than either the club or the unmoderated crowd — more strongly than anything Snowden himself adduces. It is a curious feature of his essay that it rests the case for structured deliberation on undocumented practitioner assertion when a substantial body of evidence was available to it.

The third is the experience of citizens’ assemblies, of which the Irish Citizens’ Assembly of 2016–18 is the most celebrated example. Ninety-nine randomly selected citizens, with a chair, heard expert presentations, deliberated in facilitated small groups and made recommendations on contested questions including abortion; its recommendations informed the referendum of 2018. Structurally, the assembly resembles the triopticon: expert input, followed by small-group sense-making, followed by collective formulation. But it differs in one respect that dwarfs all questions of internal design. Its members were chosen by lot. The most important variable in the politics of a room, these experiments suggest, is not how its members are arranged but who they are. Random selection addresses the club problem at its root, by ensuring that the room contains people who do not share a tradition, and addresses Young’s problem at least in part, by ensuring that it contains people of many registers. Neither Snowden’s nor Mowles’s essay discusses who is in the room or how they came to be there. Both take the composition of the gathering as given and argue about its form. The deliberative literature suggests that they have been arguing about the second most important question.

§ 6. The adversary and the frame

Chantal Mouffe’s agonistic theory of democracy, developed in The Democratic Paradox (2000) and On the Political (2005), offers the final perspective. Mouffe argued against Habermas that consensus without exclusion is impossible: every consensus is a temporary hegemony that rests on the exclusion of some alternative, and the pretence that rational deliberation can reach a consensus beyond power merely disguises the hegemony. Conflict cannot be eliminated from political life. What democratic institutions can do is transform antagonism — the relation between enemies who seek each other’s destruction — into agonism — the relation between adversaries who share a commitment to the institutions within which they contend, while disagreeing fundamentally about how those institutions should be filled.

Mouffe offers qualified support to Snowden. The triopticon’s ritualisation of opposition — its insistence on odd numbers, its appointed Coyote, its design against premature consensus — is an agonistic device: it institutionalises disagreement so that it can be expressed without becoming destructive. This is a real virtue, and one which the consensus-seeking workshop Mowles describes plainly lacks. But Mouffe’s theory contains a demand that the triopticon cannot meet. For agonism to be genuine rather than managed, the frame itself must remain contestable: the hegemony that any set of institutions embodies must be open to challenge by those it disadvantages. A Coyote who may challenge any assumption except the assumption that there should be Coyotes is not an adversary in Mouffe’s sense but a licensed critic within an uncontested order — Gluckman’s ritual of rebellion again, now in the vocabulary of political theory.

Mouffe offers qualified support to Mowles as well. His insistence that frustration and conflict are where new thinking occurs, and his reluctance to design them away, is agonistic in spirit. But agonism requires adversaries — people who genuinely disagree about fundamental matters. A conference of people who share a tradition may host vigorous argument, but it is argument among friends, within a shared hegemony; it is not the encounter of adversaries that Mouffe takes to be the life of democratic politics. The open conference, measured against Mouffe, is not too conflictual but not conflictual enough: its disagreements are those a shared tradition permits.

§ 7. What the politics of the room requires

Drawing these strands together, democratic theory suggests that a gathering adequate to the concerns all three traditions profess would need to satisfy at least five conditions, none of which either essay addresses directly. Its composition would have to include people who do not share its organisers’ tradition, ideally chosen by a procedure that does not merely reproduce existing networks. Its form would have to be declared, so that its second-dimensional power over the agenda is visible, and contestable, so that participants may challenge the form itself and not only what is said within it. Its modes of speech would have to be plural, making room for greeting, narrative and passion as well as exposition and reflection. Its conflicts would have to be agonistic, extending to the frame as well as the content. And its power relations would have to remain reversible, in Foucault’s sense, so that those disadvantaged by the game at one moment retain the means to change its terms at the next.

Part NineToward a discipline of declared form

In brief 10 min read
  • A discipline, not a method: a quality of attention that can be carried into any gathering.
  • Constraints are declared in three registers: governing, enabling and constitutive.
  • Six questions are put to any gathering: what is fixed; what has been shaped and by whom; what is presupposed; who is present and who is absent; which modes of speech count; how the form can be changed.
  • Its rhythm runs at three moments: threshold, breakdown and close, never continuously.
  • Specific demands are made of each tradition, the discipline’s own failure modes are named, and it ends in cultivation over design.

A critique that ends only in critique leaves its reader with nothing to do on Monday morning. The preceding parts have argued that neither the open room nor the designed room escapes the predicate; that the asymmetry of perception it describes is rooted in the structure of tacit knowing, the withdrawal of equipment, the incorporation of habit and the styles of thought collectives; that the classical Chinese tradition located the reconciliation of form and spontaneity not in the design of events but in the cultivation of persons; and that democratic theory requires of any gathering attention to its composition, the contestability of its form, the plurality of its modes of speech, the reach of its conflicts and the reversibility of its power relations. It remains to ask what practice might follow from all this. The answer offered here is deliberately modest. It is not a new method to be placed alongside the triopticon or the open conference; the world has enough methods, and a new one would merely become a new jacket. It is a discipline — in the older sense of a practice by which a person or community forms itself — that can be carried into any gathering, whatever its form, and that aims at a single end: that those who gather should be able to perceive, name and alter the forms that bind them.

§ 1. Why a discipline and not a method

The distinction matters, and it follows from Foucault’s formulation discussed in Part Eight. A method specifies what is to be done: a sequence of steps, a distribution of roles, a set of outputs. A discipline specifies a quality of attention that is to be brought to whatever is done. Methods can be licensed, packaged and transferred; disciplines can only be practised, and they are learned slowly, in the manner Merleau-Ponty described, by incorporation. A method is a garment; a discipline is a way of wearing garments. The present proposal is of the second kind because the argument of this disputation forbids the first. If every method is a jacket, and every jacket tends to become invisible to its wearer, then what is needed is not a better jacket but a cultivated capacity to perceive jackets as jackets — one’s own above all.

§ 2. Three registers of declaration

The first element of the discipline is the declaration of constraints in the three registers distinguished in Question II, now enriched by the intervening analysis.

Governing constraints are those that fix outcomes or fix the shape of outcomes in advance: a decision that must be reached by a certain hour, a deliverable that must be produced, a format into which contributions must be fitted. They are usually the easiest to declare and the most often concealed, because a gathering that announces in advance that its conclusions must take a certain form appears less participatory than one that does not. The discipline requires that they be stated at the outset, together with the authority from which they derive.

Enabling constraints are those that shape the conditions of interaction without determining its outcomes: roles, sequences, group sizes, rules of turn-taking, periods of silence. They are the designer’s proper domain, and the triopticon is an elaborate system of them. The discipline requires that they be declared not only as procedures but as choices — that the convener say not merely what the form is but why this form was chosen over others, and who chose it. It requires, further, that the declaration name what the form is designed to prevent and what it may, in preventing that, also suppress.

Constitutive constraints are those that make the gathering what it is: the shared tradition, vocabulary, canon, habitus and history that its members bring with them. They are the hardest to declare, because they are held in subsidiary awareness and because declaring them threatens the members’ experience of freedom. They are also the most important to declare, because they are the constraints that newcomers meet as invisible walls. The discipline requires that a gathering with a strong constitutive form make some portion of that form explicit to those who do not yet share it — its key terms, its tacit norms of interpretation and turn-taking, its characteristic ways of disagreeing — not as a set of rules to be obeyed but as an account of what the newcomer is entering.

§ 3. Six questions

The second element of the discipline is a set of questions to be asked of any gathering, whether by its convener, its participants or both. They are drawn from the analysis of the preceding parts and correspond to the conditions identified at the end of Part Eight.

First: what is fixed here, and by whose authority? This question surfaces governing constraints and the sources of their legitimacy. It is answered honestly when the convener can name the outcomes the gathering is required to produce and the person or body that requires them.

Second: what conditions have been shaped, by whom, and to prevent what? This question surfaces enabling constraints and the second-dimensional power of the designer. It is answered honestly when the designer is named and the form is justified by reference to specific dangers — entrainment, domination, premature consensus — rather than presented as neutral.

Third: what does this gathering presuppose that some of those present may lack? This question surfaces constitutive constraints and the price of entry. It is answered honestly when established members can name the vocabulary, competences and norms on which their participation depends, and can recognise that these are not universally shared.

Fourth: who is here, how did they come to be here, and who is absent? This question surfaces composition, which Part Eight identified as the most important and most neglected variable. It is answered honestly when the gathering can say by what process its membership was assembled, whom that process systematically excludes, and whether any part of the membership has been chosen by a procedure — lot, nomination by outsiders, deliberate invitation of adversaries — designed to bring in those who do not share the organisers’ tradition.

Fifth: which modes of speech does this form welcome, and which does it discount? This question surfaces third-dimensional power and Young’s concern for greeting, rhetoric and narrative. It is answered honestly when the form can be shown to make room for contributions that are not argument or reflection — for stories, for emotion, for refusal — and when established participants can recognise the registers in which they themselves are most at ease as particular rather than universal.

Sixth: by what means may participants contest and alter the form itself, during the gathering and not only afterwards? This question surfaces the reversibility of power and the contestability of the frame. It is answered honestly when there exists, within the gathering, a recognised moment or channel through which participants may propose that the form be changed, and when such proposals have some real prospect of being taken up.

§ 4. The rhythm of the audit

Polanyi’s warning, discussed in Part Six, must govern the use of these questions. To make tacit constraints focal is to disturb their functioning; a gathering that interrogated its own form continuously would never get around to anything else, and would exemplify Griffin’s doubling of process in its most exhausting form. The discipline therefore proceeds by oscillation rather than by continuous exposure. It suggests three moments in the life of a gathering at which the questions might be asked. At the threshold, before substantive work begins, the convener declares the governing and enabling constraints and offers newcomers an account of the constitutive ones. At a point of breakdown — when, in Heidegger’s sense, the form becomes conspicuous because it is failing someone — the gathering pauses to consider whether the form should be altered. And at the close, the gathering reflects on what its form made possible and what it suppressed, so that the form may be revised before the next occasion. Between these moments, the form is allowed to withdraw into use, as equipment must if any work is to be done.

§ 5. The discipline applied

The discipline yields specific demands of each of the traditions under disputation, and it is worth stating them plainly.

Of the open conference, it asks that the liturgy be named as a liturgy: that supper, keynote and open conversation be acknowledged as a form that has been chosen and could be otherwise. It asks that newcomers be offered, at the threshold, some account of the tradition’s vocabulary and its tacit norms — not to domesticate them, but to lower the price of entry. It asks that the gathering deliberately include some who do not share the tradition, and that their presence be treated not as a problem to be integrated but as the occasion of the epistemological crisis through which, on MacIntyre’s account, traditions grow. It asks that the reflexive habit of talking about how we are talking be extended to talking about who is not talking, and why. And it asks that the tradition’s confidence in emergent relating be tempered by the evidence on group polarisation, and by Bion’s account of how unstructured groups slide into basic-assumption mentality.

Of the triopticon and kindred methods, it asks that the designer be named and the form justified, not merely described. It asks that the method include a recognised moment at which participants may challenge and alter the roles and sequences themselves — that the Coyote’s licence be extended, at least once, to the frame. It asks that the method make room for narrative and for registers other than expert exposition and professional sense-making, so that participants whose knowledge is situated rather than disciplinary have somewhere to put it. It asks that the method be practised over time with the same participants, so that its forms have the chance to be incorporated rather than merely imposed — that it become, in Xunzi’s terms, a press-frame applied with the patience the image requires. And it asks that the method’s symbolic apparatus be examined for its provenance and its effects, and renamed if its names cannot bear that examination.

Of critical systems thinking, it asks that the curator of methods declare his own stance as a stance — that the meta-methodological position from which methods are selected be acknowledged as itself a jacket, with its own tacit commitments about what counts as emancipation and who counts as disadvantaged. And it asks that the selection of method be, where possible, submitted to the participants whom the method will govern, rather than made on their behalf by an expert in methods.

§ 6. How the discipline may fail

Honesty requires acknowledging that this discipline is subject to the same predicate as everything else, and may fail in identifiable ways. It may become a method: its six questions turned into a checklist, administered by consultants, licensed and branded, until it is one more jacket that its wearers no longer see. It may fall into infinite regress: the declaration of constraints requiring a declaration of the constraints on declaration, and so on, until the gathering disappears into its own reflexivity. It may become performative: constraints declared without any intention of altering them, so that the declaration serves to legitimate the form rather than expose it to challenge. And it may damage the tacit knowledge on which mature practice depends, by forcing into focal awareness what functions only when held subsidiarily.

Against these failures there is no guarantee, only the safeguards that the preceding analysis suggests: that the discipline remain a quality of attention rather than a procedure; that it proceed by oscillation, at threshold, breakdown and close, rather than continuously; that declarations be tested by whether they ever lead to change; and that it be practised by communities over time, so that its own demands can in turn be incorporated and forgotten, and rediscovered when they are needed. The discipline, in short, must be held as Cook Ding holds his knife: not abandoned, not hacked with, but moved lightly through the spaces, and slowed with great care at the knot.

§ 7. Cultivation over design

The deepest conclusion of this disputation is not about gatherings at all, but about the people who gather. Both essays under examination locate the answer to the problem of structure in the form of events: Snowden in their design, Mowles in their openness. The classical Chinese tradition, the phenomenology of habit and the hermeneutics of tradition converge on a different answer. The reconciliation of form and spontaneity is achieved, when it is achieved, in persons and communities formed over long periods to perceive the pattern in things and to move with it — in the seventy-year-old who follows his heart without overstepping the square, in the cook who after nineteen years carves along the hollows of the ox, in the organist who has made the instrument his body, in the tradition that argues with itself about its own goods and seeks out the rival who might explain its failures. No event, however designed or however open, can substitute for such formation. What events can do is either serve it or obstruct it.

The discipline of declared form is, in the end, a discipline of formation. Its aim is not better meetings but better practitioners: people capable of perceiving their own jackets, of entering others’ without contempt, of naming the walls of any room they are in, and of proposing to move them when they bind those least able to move them alone. Such people will make better use of the triopticon and of the open conference alike, because they will hold both as forms rather than as truths. That is a slower ambition than either essay proposes. It is also, this disputation submits, the only one equal to the problem both essays have identified.

The Final RulingUpon the predicate, the two essays, and the room in Hull

In brief 3 min read
  • The predicate and its twin hold against both essays.
  • Snowden is right about kind, power and choice, and wrong about “neither”.
  • Mowles is right about machines, wisdom and uncertainty, and wrong about structure.
  • Jackson is owed a hearing in his own voice.
  • Conditions are laid down for the room in Hull.

The disputation began with a predicate: to the strait-jacketed, the other always seems baggy. It has tested that predicate against Snowden’s essay and found that his title, his taxonomy, his analogies and his proposed adjudication all exhibit it: he perceives the constitutive constraint of Mowles’s tradition as bagginess because it is not designed, and he perceives his own method as a mean between extremes because he stands inside it. It has tested the predicate’s twin, to the baggy, the other always seems boxed, against Mowles’s essay and found that it holds there with equal force: he perceives the facilitated workshop as a cage because its form is alien, and his own conference as open because its form has become his body. It has examined the symbolic apparatus of the triopticon and found that its borrowed bestiary structures the room as surely as any recognised power, while presenting itself as mere play. It has refuted, clause by clause, the passage in which Snowden indicts the open room, and found that its strongest claims tell more against the designed room than against the club, and that its final observation, rightly understood, names the bias that undermines its own characterisation of the club.

It has then sought the roots of the predicate. In the phenomenology of tacit knowing, equipment and habit, it found that the constraints we inhabit are held in a mode of awareness that renders them invisible as constraints, and that form becomes visible chiefly when it fails or is foreign. In the sociology of thought collectives, it found that communities perceive their own styles as reality and others’ as arbitrary. In the hermeneutics of tradition, it found that shared prejudice enables thought so long as it remains at risk. In the philosophy of law and freedom, it found that freedom is secured where form is both owned and articulable. In the classical Chinese debate, it found the opposition of baggy and boxed already named as rustic and clerk, and its reconciliation located in the cultivated person and the bounded emptiness of the room. In democratic theory, it found that the most important questions about any gathering — who is present, whose speech counts, whether the frame may be contested, whether power remains reversible — are questions neither essay asks.

The ruling is therefore as follows. Snowden is right that structure is unavoidable, that its kind matters more than its quantity, that unstructured rooms do not abolish power, and that choosing no constraints is itself a choice; he is wrong to suppose that his method stands outside the axis it describes, wrong to treat the constitutive form of a tradition as an absence of form, and wrong to propose an adjudication conducted within his own form. Mowles is right that over-designed process can reduce its participants to compliance, right that knowledge is not wisdom, right that frustration and uncertainty are fertile, and right that machine intimacy threatens the human encounter; he is wrong to treat structure as such as the enemy of that encounter, wrong to overlook the rigorous form of the practices he most admires, and wrong to leave unanswered the question of who is protected in the room he loves. Jackson, who has spoken in neither essay in his own voice, is owed a hearing in it.

As for the room in Hull, if it is ever convened: let it be composed, at least in part, of people who belong to none of the three traditions. Let each tradition begin by describing itself as the others see it. Let the form of the gathering be declared, justified and open to challenge from the first hour. Let it make room for stories as well as arguments. Let its conflicts reach the frame. And let it be understood by all who attend that its purpose is not to determine which tradition is right, but to form, in some of those present, the capacity to see their own jacket for the first time. That would be neither baggy nor boxed. It would be a room whose walls everyone could see, and whose emptiness — bounded, declared, shared — might at last be of use.

Every gathering is clothed. The question worth asking of any room is not whether it has walls, but whether those within it can see them, name them, and, when they bind the weakest, move them together.

The rapid falling of a group into familiar patterns, often set by the first confident voice.

Bourdieu’s terms. Habitus: durable dispositions that structure conduct without conscious rule-following. Doxa: what goes without saying because it comes without saying.
